Habits

10 everyday habits that help keep your brain sharp as you age

Ten everyday habits linked with a healthier brain as you age, from walking and sleep to hearing checks and staying social, based on what major research reviews have found.

By Jude UchellaFounder, Health makes you
27 Nov 20264 min read

Most of us worry more about our brains as we get older: the forgotten name, the lost keys, the word on the tip of the tongue. Some of that is completely normal. But research is clear that everyday habits have a real effect on how well the brain ages.

"Brain" is one of the most searched health topics on Pinterest this month. Here are ten habits that major research reviews link with a healthier brain, none of which need special equipment or supplements.

Why habits matter

In 2024, the Lancet Commission on dementia, a large group of international experts, concluded that nearly half of dementia cases worldwide might be prevented or delayed by tackling 14 risk factors across a lifetime. Many of them are everyday habits and health checks. No habit is a guarantee, but together they stack the odds in your favour.

1. Move most days

Physical activity is one of the best things you can do for your brain. It improves blood flow, supports the growth of new connections and helps control blood pressure and blood sugar. Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, like brisk walking. If you're starting from zero, our 4-week walking plan is an easy way in.

2. Protect your sleep

During deep sleep, the brain clears out waste products and stores memories. Most adults need 7 to 9 hours. See our bedtime routine and watch our video on what happens to your body at night.

3. Get your hearing checked

This one surprises people. Untreated hearing loss in midlife is one of the biggest single risk factors the Lancet Commission identified. When you struggle to hear, your brain works harder and you tend to withdraw from conversation. If you're turning the TV up or asking people to repeat themselves, get a hearing test. Hearing aids, if you need them, are worth it.

4. Look after your eyes

The 2024 Lancet report added untreated vision loss to its list. Have regular eye tests and get cataracts treated if you need to.

5. Keep your blood pressure in check

What's good for your heart is good for your brain. High blood pressure in midlife damages small blood vessels in the brain. Know your numbers and treat high blood pressure if you have it.

6. Eat a brain-friendly diet

Diets rich in vegetables, leafy greens, berries, beans, whole grains, fish, nuts and olive oil, like the Mediterranean and MIND diets, are linked with slower cognitive decline. Our 7-day anti-inflammatory dinner plan follows the same pattern.

7. Stay connected

Loneliness and social isolation are linked with a higher risk of dementia. Conversation is a workout for the brain. Call a friend, join a club, volunteer, have dinner with family, or chat with neighbours.

8. Keep learning

Learning something new and challenging, like a language, an instrument, a craft, or a new game, builds what researchers call "cognitive reserve". Chess counts: we made a whole video on why your health is like a game of chess.

9. Don't smoke, and go easy on alcohol

Smoking damages blood vessels everywhere, including the brain. Drinking a lot of alcohol is also linked with brain shrinkage and dementia. If you drink, keep it moderate, and the less the better.

10. Protect your head

Head injuries raise dementia risk. Wear a helmet when cycling, wear a seatbelt, and make your home safer from falls, with good lighting, grab rails and no loose rugs, especially as you get older.

What about brain supplements and games?

There's no strong evidence that over-the-counter "brain boosting" supplements prevent memory loss in healthy people. Brain-training apps make you better at the games themselves, but there's less evidence they protect against decline in everyday life. Real-world activities that combine learning, movement and people seem to be the better bet.

When to see a doctor

Occasional forgetfulness is normal. See a doctor if memory problems are getting worse, affecting daily life (like getting lost in familiar places, struggling with bills, or repeating the same questions), or if family members are worried. Some causes of memory problems, such as vitamin B12 deficiency, thyroid problems, sleep apnoea and medication side effects, are treatable.

The bottom line

A healthier brain is built from ordinary days: walking, sleeping well, eating plenty of plants, staying social, learning new things, and keeping up with hearing, eye and blood pressure checks. It's never too early, and never too late, to start. For more, watch our video on 7 healthy aging habits.
