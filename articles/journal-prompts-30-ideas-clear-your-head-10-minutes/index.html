30 journal prompts to clear your head in 10 minutes a day

Thirty simple journal prompts for busy minds, gratitude, mornings and evenings, plus what research says about writing things down and an easy way to make journaling a daily habit.

By Jude UchellaFounder, Health makes you
26 Nov 20265 min read

Some days your head feels like a browser with 40 tabs open. Work, family, money, the thing someone said at lunch, the email you forgot to send. Journaling is one of the simplest ways to close a few of those tabs, and it's having a big moment: "journal prompts" is one of the most searched health topics on Pinterest every month.

The hardest part is usually the blank page. What do you actually write? That's what prompts are for. Below you'll find 30 of them, grouped by what you need that day, plus a simple way to make ten minutes of writing a habit that sticks.

Why writing things down helps

There's nothing magic about a notebook, but there is something useful about getting thoughts out of your head and onto paper. The University of Rochester Medical Center describes journaling as simply writing down your thoughts and feelings to understand them more clearly. It lists managing anxiety, reducing stress and coping with low mood among the ways a journal can help, partly because writing helps you sort your problems and worries by priority, notice what triggers them, and catch negative thoughts so you can answer them with kinder ones.

Gratitude writing has some interesting research behind it too. Harvard Health Publishing describes a study by psychologists Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough in which participants wrote a few sentences each week. One group wrote about things they were grateful for, one about daily irritations, and one about events with no particular slant. After 10 weeks, the gratitude group felt more optimistic and better about their lives. They also exercised more and had fewer visits to the doctor than the group who wrote about irritations.

Journaling isn't a cure for anything, and it won't replace support from people around you or a professional when life is genuinely hard. But as a daily ten-minute habit, it's cheap, private and easy to start.

How to start (and keep going)

Keep it small. Ten minutes is plenty. A few honest sentences count. You don't need to fill a page.

Pick a regular time. Many people like the morning, before the day gets noisy, or the evening as part of winding down. The University of Rochester suggests trying to write every day and keeping a pen and paper handy so you can jot things down whenever you need to.

Forget the rules. Spelling, grammar and neat handwriting don't matter. Nobody else needs to read it. Bullet points, lists and half-sentences are all fine.

Use paper if you can. You can journal on your phone, but a notebook keeps you away from notifications and makes the habit feel like a break from screens.

Choose one prompt. Don't try to answer five. Pick the one that matches your mood today and write until the timer goes off.

8 prompts for when your mind is busy

  1. What's taking up the most space in my head right now?
  2. If I could take one thing off my plate this week, what would it be, and what's stopping me?
  3. What am I worrying about that I can't actually control?
  4. What's one small thing I can do in the next 24 hours to make tomorrow easier?
  5. Write down everything you need to remember, then circle the three that really matter.
  6. What would I tell a friend who was dealing with what I'm dealing with?
  7. What is something I've been putting off, and how would it feel to finish it?
  8. What drained my energy today, and what gave me energy?

8 gratitude prompts

  1. What are three things that went well today, however small?
  2. Who made my life easier this week, and how could I thank them?
  3. What's something about my body I'm grateful for today?
  4. What's a simple pleasure I enjoyed recently, like a good meal, a song or a warm bed?
  5. What's something I have now that I once hoped for?
  6. Which place makes me feel calm, and why?
  7. What's a lesson a hard time taught me that I'm glad to have learned?
  8. Who is someone I haven't spoken to in a while who made a difference in my life?

7 morning prompts

  1. How do I want to feel by the end of today?
  2. What's the one task that would make today a good day if I got it done?
  3. What can I do today that my future self will thank me for?
  4. How will I move my body today, even for ten minutes?
  5. What am I looking forward to today?
  6. What would make today 10% better?
  7. What's one kind thing I can do for someone today?

7 evening prompts

  1. What did I learn today?
  2. When did I feel most like myself today?
  3. What's one thing I'd do differently if I had today again?
  4. What am I proud of, even if no one else noticed?
  5. What can I let go of before I sleep tonight?
  6. What's one thing I want to remember about today?
  7. What am I looking forward to tomorrow?

Making it part of your routine

The easiest way to keep journaling is to attach it to something you already do. Write while your coffee brews in the morning, during a quiet lunch break, or as the last thing before bed. Evening writing pairs especially well with a wind-down routine, because putting worries on paper can make them feel less urgent when your head hits the pillow. Our 60-minute bedtime routine shows where a few minutes of writing fits into the hour before sleep.

If you miss a day, don't try to catch up. Just open the notebook the next day and answer a single prompt. A habit you return to imperfectly is better than a perfect one you drop after a week.

When to reach out

Journaling can bring difficult feelings to the surface. That's normal, and often helpful. But if you notice that you're feeling low most days, struggling to cope, or having thoughts of harming yourself, please don't rely on a notebook alone. Talk to someone you trust or your doctor. In the US, you can call or text 988 at any time to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

The bottom line

You don't need a fancy notebook, perfect handwriting or an hour a day. Ten minutes and one honest question are enough to clear some mental clutter, notice what's going well, and head into the day or the night a little lighter. Pick one prompt from this list tonight and see how it feels.
