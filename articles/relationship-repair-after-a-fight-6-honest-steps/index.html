How to repair a relationship after a fight, in 6 honest steps

Six practical steps to repair a relationship after an argument, from cooling down to a real apology, plus what research on couples says about repair and when to get outside help.

By Jude UchellaFounder, Health makes you
27 Nov 20263 min read

Every couple argues. Every close friendship and family has its clashes. What matters isn't whether you fight, but what happens afterwards. Searches for "relationship repair" on Pinterest have tripled since last year, and with the holidays bringing families together, it's a timely skill.

Relationships matter for health too. Strong, supportive relationships are linked with lower stress, better mental health and even a longer life. Here are six honest steps for making things right after a fight.

Why repair matters more than avoiding conflict

Relationship researchers John and Julie Gottman studied thousands of couples over decades. One of their key findings was that happy couples still argue. What sets them apart is that they make "repair attempts" during and after conflict, small moves that calm things down and reconnect, and that their partner accepts them.

A repair attempt can be as small as a joke, a touch on the arm, "I'm sorry, that came out wrong", or "Can we start again?"

Step 1: Take a real break if you need one

When an argument gets heated, your heart races and your body floods with stress hormones. In that state, it's very hard to listen or think clearly. The Gottmans suggest taking a break of at least 20 minutes to calm down when either person feels overwhelmed.

The key is to say when you'll come back: "I need half an hour to calm down, then I want to talk about this properly." Walking away without a word feels like rejection.

Use the break to actually relax: a walk, a shower, slow breathing. Not to rehearse your argument.

Step 2: Get curious about their side

Before you talk again, ask yourself: what was this really about for them? Many fights about dishes or money or in-laws are really about feeling unappreciated, unheard or worried.

When you talk, ask questions and listen without interrupting. Try summing up what you heard: "So you felt like I didn't take it seriously when you said you were tired." You don't have to agree to understand.

Step 3: Own your part

There's almost always something you could have done differently: your tone, the timing, something you said in the heat of the moment. Name it clearly.

"I raised my voice, and I'm sorry" is far more powerful than a long explanation of why you did it.

Step 4: Make a real apology

A real apology is specific, takes responsibility and doesn't come with a "but". Compare:

  • "I'm sorry if you were upset." (Not an apology.)
  • "I'm sorry I brought up your mum in front of the kids. That was unfair, and I can see it hurt you." (An apology.)

Step 5: Talk about what you each need

Once you both feel calmer and heard, talk about what would help next time. Use "I" statements rather than blame: "I feel overwhelmed when the house is messy at the end of the day. Could we do a ten-minute tidy together after dinner?" works better than "You never help."

Agree on one small, concrete change, not a list.

Step 6: Reconnect

After a fight, do something that reminds you you're on the same team: a hug, a walk, a cup of tea together, a silly film. Small, everyday kindness builds a cushion of goodwill that makes the next disagreement easier.

When to get help

Some patterns need more than good intentions. Consider a counsellor or couples therapist if the same fights repeat without resolution, if contempt (eye-rolling, mocking, name-calling) has crept in, or if either of you is shutting down completely.

If you ever feel afraid of your partner, or there's any physical violence, threats, or controlling behaviour, that's not a normal conflict. Contact a domestic violence helpline or your doctor for confidential support. In the U.S., the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233.

Writing things down can help you sort through feelings before a hard conversation. Try a few of our 30 journal prompts to clear your head.

The bottom line

Healthy relationships aren't fight-free. They're good at repair. Calm down first, listen to understand, own your part, apologise properly, agree one small change, and then reconnect. Practised over time, these steps make relationships stronger, not weaker.
