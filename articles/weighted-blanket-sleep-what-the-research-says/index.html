Sleep

Can a weighted blanket help you sleep? What the research actually says

What a weighted blanket is, what studies have found about sleep and anxiety, how heavy yours should be, who should avoid one, and how to make it part of a better bedtime routine.

By Jude UchellaFounder, Health makes you
27 Nov 20264 min read

As the nights get longer and colder, searches for weighted blankets on Pinterest have doubled in a month. These heavy blankets, filled with glass beads or plastic pellets, promise a feeling like a gentle hug and a calmer, deeper night's sleep.

But do they actually work, or are they just an expensive comfort item? Here's what the research says, how to choose one, and who should skip them.

What is a weighted blanket?

A weighted blanket looks like a normal blanket or duvet, but it's filled with small beads sewn into pockets so the weight spreads evenly. Adult blankets usually weigh between 5 and 13 kilograms (about 10 to 30 pounds). Some are knitted from thick, heavy yarn instead of using beads.

The idea comes from something called deep pressure stimulation. Firm, even pressure on the body, a bit like a hug or being tucked in tightly, is thought to have a calming effect for some people. Occupational therapists have used weighted products for years, especially with children who have sensory processing difficulties.

What the research says

The evidence is promising but still limited.

The best-known study was a randomised controlled trial from Sweden, published in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine in 2020. It followed 120 adults with insomnia who also had conditions such as depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder or ADHD. Half slept with a weighted chain blanket for four weeks, and half used a much lighter blanket. The people using the weighted blanket reported a bigger drop in insomnia severity, along with better daytime energy and less tiredness, and many kept using it when the study ended.

That's encouraging, but it's one study in a specific group of people. Other research has been smaller, shorter, or relied on how people said they felt rather than objective sleep measures. So the honest answer is: weighted blankets help some people feel calmer and sleep better, they don't work for everyone, and they're not a treatment for sleep disorders.

How heavy should it be?

A common guideline from manufacturers is to choose a blanket that's roughly 10% of your body weight. So someone who weighs 70 kilograms (about 155 pounds) would look at a blanket of around 7 kilograms (about 15 pounds). Comfort matters more than the exact number. You should be able to move it and get out from under it easily.

Also think about:

  • Size. A weighted blanket should cover your body, not hang over the edge of the bed. If it slides off, it pulls on you all night.
  • Temperature. They can be warm. Look for breathable cotton or bamboo covers, or a knitted design, if you sleep hot.
  • Washing. Heavy blankets are hard to wash. A removable cover makes life much easier.

Who should avoid a weighted blanket

Weighted blankets aren't safe for everyone.

  • Babies and young children. Never use one for a baby or toddler. A heavy blanket can trap a small child or restrict breathing. Safe sleep guidance for infants is a firm, flat surface with no loose bedding at all.
  • People who can't easily move it off. This includes some older adults, people with limited mobility or strength, and anyone recovering from surgery.
  • People with certain health conditions. If you have sleep apnoea or other breathing problems, circulation problems, or a heart or lung condition, talk to your doctor first.
  • People who feel trapped or anxious under weight. Some people find the pressure uncomfortable rather than calming, and that's fine.

Make it part of a bigger plan

A weighted blanket works best as one piece of a good sleep routine, not as a fix on its own. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends that adults get at least 7 hours of sleep a night, and suggests going to bed and getting up at the same time each day, keeping your bedroom quiet, dark and cool, and avoiding large meals, alcohol and caffeine before bed.

You can also try the blanket on the sofa first. Many people use theirs in the evening while they read or watch TV to wind down, then sleep under a normal duvet.

When to see a doctor

If you regularly struggle to fall asleep or stay asleep, wake up exhausted, or your partner says you snore loudly or stop breathing at night, don't rely on a blanket. Speak to a doctor. Conditions like insomnia and sleep apnoea are common and treatable, and a blanket can't diagnose or fix them.

The bottom line

A weighted blanket may help you feel calmer and sleep a little better, and there's some good research to back that up for people with insomnia. It's not a miracle cure, and it's not safe for babies, young children or anyone who can't easily move it. If you're curious, choose one that's around 10% of your body weight, try it alongside a steady bedtime routine, and see how you feel after a few weeks.
