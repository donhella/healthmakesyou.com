6 benefits of broccoli sprouts (and why they beat regular broccoli)

The full guide

Almost all the benefits of broccoli sprouts come from one natural compound, and a small handful of sprouts can give you far more of it than a whole plate of regular broccoli. Here are the six benefits, why sprouts beat the broccoli you already eat, how much to have, who should be careful, and the common kitchen mistake that wipes out most of the benefit.

Why sprouts beat regular broccoli

The compound behind the benefits is called sulforaphane. In 1997, a team at Johns Hopkins University found that three-day-old broccoli sprouts contain 20 to 50 times more of the plant compound that becomes sulforaphane than mature broccoli does. A small handful of sprouts can match a big pile of broccoli.

There's a second reason. That plant compound only turns into sulforaphane when it meets a natural enzyme in the plant, which happens when you chew, chop or crush it. Cooking destroys the enzyme, and broccoli is usually cooked. Sprouts are usually eaten raw, so they win twice: more of the good stuff, eaten in the form that actually releases it.

1. They help switch on your body's own defence system

Inside every cell is a kind of master switch that turns on the genes for repairing damage and clearing out harmful substances. Sulforaphane is one of the strongest food compounds known to flip it.

Researchers saw this in a real-world trial in Qidong, China, an area with heavy air pollution. In the study, published in 2014, people who drank a broccoli sprout drink every day for 12 weeks cleared about 61% more benzene, a chemical from traffic fumes, through their urine than people who didn't get the drink. This isn't a detox-tea fantasy. It's your liver and cells doing their normal job a little better.

2. They calm long-term inflammation

Short bursts of inflammation help you heal a cut or fight an infection. The low, constant kind that lingers for years is the problem, and it's tied to heart disease, joint pain, diabetes and ageing.

In a 2019 study from Spain, overweight but otherwise healthy adults ate 30 grams of fresh broccoli sprouts a day, roughly a small handful, for ten weeks. Two inflammation markers in their blood went down, and stayed lower for a while even after they stopped. A small daily habit made a measurable difference.

3. They may help with blood sugar

In 2017, researchers in Sweden gave a concentrated broccoli sprout extract to overweight people with type 2 diabetes whose blood sugar wasn't well controlled. After 12 weeks, their fasting blood sugar came down noticeably compared with people given a dummy powder.

To be clear, that was a strong extract, not a sprinkle on a salad, and it was added to their normal treatment rather than replacing it. Still, it shows the compound can help the body handle sugar, which is one more reason to add sprouts to your meals if diabetes runs in your family.

4. They may support your heart

In a small Japanese study, people who ate broccoli sprouts every day for just one week saw their LDL cholesterol (the kind most people worry about) go down, while HDL, the helpful kind, rose a little. A study in Iran found that people with type 2 diabetes who took broccoli sprout powder had lower triglycerides, another blood fat linked to heart disease.

These are small studies, so they shouldn't be oversold. But alongside the drop in inflammation, they help explain why researchers keep coming back to this little food.

5. They support your stomach and gut

Many people carry a bacterium called H. pylori in their stomach without knowing it. It's a major cause of stomach ulcers and, over many years, raises the risk of stomach cancer.

In a 2009 study from Japan, people with H. pylori ate 70 grams of broccoli sprouts a day for eight weeks. Markers of the infection dropped, and so did inflammation in the stomach lining. When they stopped eating the sprouts, the numbers slowly crept back up. The sprouts didn't cure the infection, and anyone who has it still needs proper treatment, but they helped keep it in check. Sprouts also provide fibre, which feeds the friendly bacteria in your gut.

6. They help protect your cells

Sulforaphane is one of the most studied food compounds in cancer research. In the lab it helps cells repair damage and helps the body clear substances that can harm DNA, and population studies link eating plenty of cruciferous vegetables, the broccoli and cabbage family, with a lower risk of some cancers.

The honest part: broccoli sprouts are not a cancer cure, and nobody should skip treatment for them. Think of them as one more brick in a wall of protection, alongside not smoking, staying active and eating plenty of vegetables.

How much should you eat?

The studies above used about 30 to 70 grams of fresh sprouts a day. Thirty grams is a small handful, or about half a cup lightly packed, and that's enough. Start with a spoonful or two a day, because some people get gas or bloating at first, then build up to a handful.

Easy ways to eat them:

  • On top of a salad
  • Inside a sandwich or wrap
  • On avocado toast
  • Blended into a smoothie

Chew them well, because chewing is what mixes the plant compound with its enzyme. Some research also suggests that freezing sprouts and then blending them releases even more sulforaphane, because ice crystals break open the cells, so a frozen batch for morning smoothies works well.

Who needs to be careful?

Raw sprouts of any kind have a history of food poisoning, because the warm, damp conditions sprouts need to grow also suit bacteria like salmonella and E. coli. The US Food and Drug Administration advises pregnant women, young children, older adults and anyone with a weakened immune system to avoid raw sprouts.

If you grow your own in a jar, use seeds sold for sprouting, rinse them twice a day, keep everything clean, and throw them out if they smell bad or look slimy. If you have a thyroid condition, normal food amounts aren't a concern for most people, but check with your doctor before eating large amounts every day. And if you take a blood thinner such as warfarin, keep your intake steady, because sprouts contain vitamin K.

The mistake that wipes out the benefit

Many people buy broccoli sprouts and then cook them: stirred into hot soup, fried with eggs or steamed like broccoli. Heat destroys the enzyme that makes sulforaphane, so most of the benefit is gone before the food reaches your mouth. Eat them raw, or add them to hot food at the very end, once it's on your plate.

If you love cooked broccoli, there's a simple fix too. Sprinkle a little mustard powder over it, or top it with a few raw sprouts. Both contain the same kind of enzyme, so they bring the missing piece back.

The bottom line

Broccoli sprouts help switch on your body's own defences, calm inflammation, may help with blood sugar and cholesterol, support your stomach and gut, and are one of the most promising foods for protecting your cells. They beat regular broccoli because they carry far more of the key compound and you eat them raw. A small handful a day is all the studies used. Chew them well, keep them clean, and don't cook them.
