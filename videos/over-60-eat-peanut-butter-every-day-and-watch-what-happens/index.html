Over 60? Eat peanut butter every day and watch what happens to your body

The full guide

Peanut butter is cheap, tasty and lasts for months in the cupboard. After 60, it can be more useful than most people realise, because some of what it offers, especially protein, healthy fats and easy calories, matters even more as we age. Here's what a daily spoonful of peanut butter can do for you after 60, and how to make it part of a balanced diet.

Why the protein matters

From around middle age, the body slowly loses muscle, and that loss speeds up in later life. Doctors call it sarcopenia, and it can lead to weakness, poorer balance and a higher risk of falls. Older adults also tend to use protein less efficiently, which is why many experts suggest they need at least as much protein as younger people, and often more.

Two tablespoons of peanut butter give you around 7 to 8 grams of protein. That isn't a complete solution on its own, but spread on wholegrain toast with a glass of milk, it turns a light breakfast into one that actually helps your muscles. Pair it with regular strength exercise, such as standing up from a chair repeatedly or carrying shopping, for the best results.

The role of healthy fats

Most of the fat in peanut butter is unsaturated, the kind that helps keep cholesterol levels healthy when it replaces saturated fat. Nuts and peanuts have been linked with better heart health in large studies. One major study published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2013, following more than 100,000 people, found that those who ate nuts regularly had a lower risk of dying from heart disease and other causes over the study period.

Peanuts are technically legumes, but they have a similar nutrient profile to nuts and were included in that research.

Taste and personal preference

One of peanut butter's quiet advantages after 60 is simply that people enjoy it. Appetite often fades with age, and food can become less appealing as taste and smell change. A food that's tasty, familiar and easy to eat can make a real difference to how much nutrition someone gets. Smooth or crunchy, on toast, crackers or apple slices, there are plenty of ways to enjoy it.

Why it can help you feel fuller

The combination of protein, fat and fibre in peanut butter slows digestion and keeps you satisfied for longer. For people trying to manage their weight, a small amount can curb snacking between meals.

For older adults who struggle to eat enough, it works the other way. Peanut butter packs a lot of calories and nutrients into a small portion, around 190 calories in two tablespoons, which makes it an easy way to avoid unplanned weight loss when appetite is low.

Nutrients linked to brain health

Peanut butter contains vitamin E, an antioxidant that helps protect cells, including brain cells, from damage. It also provides niacin (vitamin B3), magnesium and folate, which all play roles in normal brain and nerve function. No single food prevents memory loss, but diets rich in nuts, legumes, vegetables and healthy fats, like the Mediterranean diet, have been linked with healthier ageing of the brain.

Making peanut butter part of a balanced diet

  • Choose the simplest jar. Look for peanuts and a little salt on the ingredients list, with no added sugar or palm oil.
  • Watch the portion. Two tablespoons a day is plenty. It's easy to overeat straight from the jar.
  • Pair it well. Wholegrain toast, oats, apple or banana slices, or celery sticks add fibre and vitamins.
  • Stir natural varieties. The oil separates; stir it back in and keep the jar in the fridge if you like it thicker.

Who should be careful?

  • Peanut allergy: anyone with a peanut allergy must avoid peanut butter completely.
  • Swallowing difficulties: thick, sticky peanut butter can be a choking risk for people who have trouble swallowing. Spread it thinly, or mix it into porridge or smoothies.
  • Weight and calories: it's calorie-dense, so measure portions if you're watching your weight.
  • Salt: choose low-salt or no-salt-added versions if you have high blood pressure.
  • Blood thinners and kidney disease: peanut butter is fine for most people, but anyone on a special diet should follow their doctor's or dietitian's advice.

The bottom line

After 60, peanut butter can be a simple daily helper: protein for your muscles, unsaturated fats for your heart, vitamin E and B vitamins for your brain and nerves, and easy calories when appetite is low. Choose a jar with just peanuts and salt, keep portions to about two tablespoons, pair it with wholegrain foods and fruit, and enjoy it. For more food ideas after 60, see Best foods for seniors.
