Blood sugar balance: eat in this order to stop spikes after meals

The full guide

You finish lunch, and an hour later your eyes are heavy, your head feels foggy and you're already looking for something sweet. Very often, that's your blood sugar shooting up and then dropping just as fast. One of the simplest ways to calm that rollercoaster has nothing to do with cutting out your favourite foods. It's about the order you eat them in. Here's the exact order for your plate, what to do in the minutes after eating, and the normal numbers to aim for.

What happens after a meal

When you eat bread, rice, pasta, potatoes or anything sweet, your body breaks the carbohydrates down into glucose, which moves into your blood. Your pancreas releases insulin to move that sugar into your cells. When a lot of glucose arrives at once, insulin has to work hard and fast, and sometimes it overshoots, so your blood sugar dips lower than where it started.

That dip is the crash: tiredness, brain fog, shaky hunger and cravings for something sweet. An occasional spike is normal. The problem is when it happens meal after meal for years, because cells can gradually stop responding to insulin as well. That's insulin resistance, the road to prediabetes and type 2 diabetes.

The order: vegetables, protein, then carbs

  1. Vegetables first: salad, greens, cooked vegetables or vegetable soup.
  2. Protein and fats next: chicken, fish, eggs, beans or meat.
  3. Starchy carbs last: rice, bread, potatoes or plantain.

It's exactly the same meal. You're only changing the sequence.

The research behind it

Researchers at Weill Cornell Medicine in New York tested this in people with type 2 diabetes, who ate the same meal on two different days. When the carbohydrates came last, blood sugar was about 30% lower half an hour after eating and close to 40% lower at one hour. Later studies found a similar effect in people with prediabetes. Same food, same amount, very different result.

Why it works

Fibre from vegetables forms a gel in your gut that slows how quickly food moves through. Protein and fat slow your stomach too, and prompt your gut to release hormones that help insulin work and keep you full. By the time the bread or rice arrives, it lands in a stomach that's already busy, so the sugar trickles into your blood instead of rushing in. A trickle means a smaller rise, a gentler fall and no crash.

When meals don't come in courses

Jollof rice, a sandwich or a bowl of pasta doesn't separate neatly, and that's fine. You don't have to be perfect:

  • Start with a small side salad or a few raw vegetables before the main dish.
  • Have a boiled egg or a handful of nuts before a carb-heavy breakfast.
  • Eat the chicken off the top of your rice before you dig into the rice.

Even a few minutes' head start for vegetables and protein helps.

Move for ten minutes after you eat

Your muscles are the biggest users of sugar in your body, and when they're moving they can pull glucose from your blood without needing much insulin. A 2022 review found that even two to five minutes of light walking after a meal lowered the blood sugar rise, and longer walks do more.

You don't need a gym. Walk around the house, wash the dishes standing up, take a slow lap round the block, or climb the stairs a few times.

If you exercise in the evening and blood sugar is your main concern, eating first and moving within the next hour tends to work well. If a big meal before training feels heavy, have a lighter, earlier dinner and keep anything afterwards built around protein and vegetables.

A simple extra: diluted vinegar

Some people add a tablespoon of vinegar, such as apple cider vinegar, to a big glass of water before a meal. Small studies suggest it can take the edge off the rise after a starchy meal. It's no substitute for food order and movement. Always dilute it, because neat vinegar is hard on your teeth and throat, and skip it if it upsets your stomach.

What about fruit?

Whole fruit isn't the enemy. Its fibre, water and nutrients slow down the sugar it contains. Berries, apples, pears, oranges and kiwi are good choices. Eat fruit with something, like an apple with nuts or berries with plain yoghurt, or at the end of a meal rather than alone on an empty stomach.

Juice and smoothies are different. Juicing removes most of the fibre, and you can drink the sugar of four or five oranges in under a minute. Eat your fruit; don't drink it.

The numbers to aim for

  • Fasting blood sugar: normal is 70 to 99 mg/dL (about 3.9 to 5.5 mmol/L). 100 to 125 mg/dL is prediabetes.
  • Two hours after a meal: usually back under 140 mg/dL (about 7.8 mmol/L).
  • HbA1c (three-month average): below 5.7% is normal, 5.7 to 6.4% is prediabetes, 6.5% and above is diabetes.

You don't need to chase a perfect number. A result of 5.5 or 5.6% is normal. Aim for steady, not zero.

Sleep, stress and muscle

Food isn't the whole picture. Even one short night can make your body less sensitive to insulin the next day, so the same breakfast gives a bigger spike. Stress releases cortisol, which tells your liver to put more sugar into your blood. And the more muscle you carry, the more places sugar has to go, which is why strength exercises like squats from a chair, wall push-ups or carrying shopping help, especially with age. Many women find blood sugar harder to control around menopause, as falling oestrogen makes the body a little less responsive to insulin, so keeping muscle matters even more then.

If you take diabetes medication

If you take insulin or tablets that lower blood sugar, talk to your doctor before making big changes to how you eat or exercise. These habits can lower your sugar, and combined with medication that can sometimes push it too low. Your doctor may need to adjust your dose.

The bottom line

Start with your vegetables, then protein and fats, and leave starchy carbs for last. Move for ten minutes after eating. Eat fruit whole and with something, not as juice. Look after your sleep, manage stress and give your muscles work to do. None of this means giving up foods you enjoy; it helps your body handle them better, and the after-meal crashes start to fade.
