The benefits of almond milk you never knew

The full guide

Almond milk is one of the most popular plant milks in the world, and many people reach for it simply because it's dairy-free. But what does it actually do for your body, and where does it fall short? Here's an honest look at almond milk's benefits, from bones and heart health to vitamin E and weight, plus what to check on the carton before you buy.

A lighter alternative to regular milk

Unsweetened almond milk is very low in calories, usually around 30 to 40 calories a cup, compared with roughly 90 for skimmed cow milk and about 150 for whole milk. It's made by blending almonds with water and straining out the solids, so most of the carton is water.

That lightness is both its strength and its weakness. It's an easy swap if you want fewer calories in your coffee, cereal or smoothie, but it also means almond milk is low in protein, usually only about 1 gram a cup compared with 8 grams in cow milk. If you switch, make sure you're getting protein from other foods such as eggs, beans, fish, yoghurt or nuts.

Naturally lactose-free

Because it's made from nuts, almond milk contains no lactose at all. That makes it a comfortable choice for people with lactose intolerance, who get bloating, gas or cramps after dairy. It's also free of cow's milk proteins, so it can suit people with a cow's milk allergy, as long as they aren't allergic to tree nuts.

That last point matters. Anyone with a tree nut allergy must avoid almond milk completely. And almond milk is not a suitable main drink for babies or young children, because it's far too low in protein, fat and calories for a growing child.

What it can do for your bones

Almonds themselves contain some calcium, but very little of it ends up in homemade almond milk. Most shop-bought brands add calcium, often bringing it to a level similar to cow milk, and many also add vitamin D, which helps your body absorb calcium.

So almond milk can support your bones, but only if it's fortified. Check the label for calcium and vitamin D. Homemade and "organic" versions are often unfortified, because organic rules in some countries don't allow added nutrients. The NHS recommends choosing unsweetened, calcium-fortified versions of dairy alternatives.

A heart-friendly choice

Almond milk is naturally free of cholesterol and very low in saturated fat. The small amount of fat it does contain comes mostly from unsaturated fats, the type the NHS recommends choosing over saturated fat to help keep cholesterol levels healthy.

Whole almonds have stronger evidence for heart health, because a handful gives you far more of their fats, fibre and plant compounds than a glass of almond milk. Think of almond milk as a heart-friendly base rather than a heart medicine.

The vitamin E advantage

Almonds are one of the richest food sources of vitamin E, an antioxidant that helps protect your cells from damage and supports healthy skin and eyes. Many almond milks contain a useful amount, and some brands add more. It's one area where almond milk naturally outperforms cow milk, which contains very little vitamin E.

Almond milk and weight management

Because unsweetened almond milk is so low in calories, swapping it in for whole milk in a daily latte or cereal can trim calories over time. That small change can help if you're trying to manage your weight.

The catch is sugar. Sweetened and flavoured almond milks, especially vanilla and chocolate, can contain several teaspoons of added sugar per glass, which cancels out the advantage. Always choose the carton that says "unsweetened", and remember that low protein means it won't keep you as full as dairy milk.

A benefit for kidney-conscious diets

People with chronic kidney disease are often told to limit phosphorus and potassium, both of which are high in cow milk. Unfortified almond milk is naturally lower in both, which is why it's sometimes suggested in kidney-friendly diets.

But check the label: some almond milks have phosphate additives, which the body absorbs very easily. If you have kidney disease, follow the advice of your kidney team or dietitian on which milk is right for you.

What to look for at the shop

  • Unsweetened: no added sugar.
  • Fortified: with calcium, vitamin D and ideally vitamin B12.
  • Short ingredient list: almonds, water, and the added nutrients.
  • Check additives: if you have kidney disease, avoid phosphate additives.
  • Shake well: added calcium settles at the bottom of the carton.

The bottom line

Almond milk is a light, lactose-free, low-calorie milk that's naturally rich in vitamin E and low in saturated fat. Fortified versions can support your bones, and unsweetened versions can help with weight. But it's very low in protein and only as nutritious as what's added to it, so read the label, choose unsweetened and fortified, and get your protein elsewhere.
