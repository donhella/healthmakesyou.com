11 Side Effects of Eating Too Much Cheese

The full guide

Cheese is one of life's great pleasures, from melted mozzarella on pizza to a sharp cheddar on toast. It's also a good source of protein and calcium. But cheese is rich, salty and easy to overeat, and too much of it can catch up with you. Here are eleven side effects of eating too much cheese, which ones are real, which are mostly myth, and how to keep enjoying it.

1. Your heart and cholesterol

Many cheeses are high in saturated fat. Eating a lot of saturated fat can raise LDL cholesterol, the type that builds up in arteries and increases the risk of heart disease. The NHS advises keeping saturated fat to no more than 30 grams a day for men and 20 grams for women, and a 30-gram chunk of cheddar alone contains around 6 grams.

The picture is a little more nuanced than once thought: some research suggests cheese may not raise heart risk as much as its fat content would suggest. But large portions every day still add up.

2. Weight gain

Cheese is calorie-dense. A matchbox-sized 30-gram piece of cheddar has about 120 calories, and it's very easy to eat two or three times that while grating it over pasta or snacking from the block. Over time, those extra calories can lead to weight gain.

3. Lactose intolerance

Fresh, soft cheeses like ricotta, cottage cheese and cream cheese contain more lactose than aged hard cheeses. For people with lactose intolerance, large amounts can cause bloating, gas, cramps and diarrhoea. The good news is that hard cheeses such as cheddar, parmesan and gouda contain very little lactose, so many lactose-intolerant people can enjoy them in normal portions.

4. Skin and acne

Some studies have linked dairy, mainly milk, to acne, particularly in teenagers. The evidence for cheese specifically is weaker. If you notice your skin breaks out when you eat a lot of dairy, try cutting back for a few weeks and see whether it makes a difference.

5. Constipation

Cheese contains no fibre, and meals built around cheese, like pizza, toasties and macaroni cheese, often lack vegetables and whole grains too. A diet heavy in cheese and low in fibre can lead to constipation. Pair cheese with high-fibre foods and drink plenty of water.

6. High blood pressure

Cheese is one of the bigger sources of salt in many diets. Halloumi, feta, blue cheese and processed cheese slices are especially salty. Too much salt raises blood pressure, so if yours is high, choose lower-salt cheeses like Swiss-style or fresh mozzarella and keep portions small.

7. Your teeth: actually good news

This one is often listed as a side effect, but cheese is generally good for teeth. It's low in sugar, and eating cheese can help neutralise acid in the mouth and provide calcium and phosphate for enamel. The real risk is what cheese is eaten with, like sweet crackers or sugary chutneys.

8. Dairy allergy

A cow's milk allergy is different from lactose intolerance. It's an immune reaction to milk proteins and can cause hives, swelling, vomiting or breathing problems. People with a milk allergy need to avoid cheese made from cow's milk, and usually goat and sheep cheese too, because the proteins are so similar.

9. Congestion: mostly a myth

Many people believe dairy makes them produce more mucus. Research doesn't really support this: a 2005 review in the Journal of the American College of Nutrition concluded that milk doesn't increase mucus production. Some people do notice that dairy makes their saliva feel thicker, which can feel like congestion. If it bothers you, cut back and see.

10. Achy joints and bones

There's no good evidence that cheese harms your bones; in fact its calcium and protein help them. Very salty diets can increase the amount of calcium lost in urine, so moderation matters, but cheese in normal amounts supports bone health. For gout, dairy is generally considered fine and low-fat dairy may even help.

11. Disturbed sleep

The idea that cheese gives you nightmares is a myth. But a large, rich, fatty meal late at night can cause indigestion or heartburn, which can keep you awake. Aged cheeses also contain tyramine, which can trigger migraines in some people. If you're prone to either, avoid big cheesy meals close to bedtime.

How much cheese is sensible?

Most guidelines suggest a portion of about 30 grams, roughly the size of a matchbox or two thumbs. Enjoying a portion a day as part of a balanced diet is fine for most people.

  • Grate hard cheese finely: it spreads further.
  • Choose strong-flavoured cheeses, so a little goes a long way.
  • Try reduced-fat or lower-salt versions.
  • Pair cheese with fruit, vegetables and whole grains.

The bottom line

Cheese gives you protein and calcium, and in sensible portions it's a healthy part of most diets. The side effects come from eating too much: saturated fat, salt, calories and, for some people, lactose or a milk allergy. A few common fears, like congestion and bad dreams, are mostly myths. Keep portions to about a matchbox size, choose strong flavours and lower-salt options, and enjoy it.
