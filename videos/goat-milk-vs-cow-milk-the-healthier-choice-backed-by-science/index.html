Goat Milk vs Cow Milk: The Healthier Choice Backed by Science

The full guide

Goat milk vs cow milk is one of the most common questions people ask when they start looking at their diet more closely. Is goat milk genuinely healthier, or just more expensive? The honest answer is that each one wins in different areas. Here's a side-by-side look at nutrition, digestion, allergies, skin and gut health, so you can decide which suits you.

Nutrition: closer than you'd think

Per cup (about 245 ml), goat and cow milk are surprisingly similar. Both give you roughly 8 to 9 grams of protein, plus calcium, phosphorus and potassium.

Where goat milk comes out ahead:

  • Slightly more calcium, potassium and vitamin A
  • A higher share of medium-chain fatty acids, which the body absorbs and uses for energy quickly

Where cow milk comes out ahead:

  • More folate and vitamin B12
  • Often fortified with vitamin D, depending on the country
  • Fewer calories if you choose semi-skimmed or skimmed, which are easy to find

So on nutrition alone, it's close to a draw. Goat milk is a little richer; cow milk covers folate and B12 better.

Digestion: goat milk's biggest advantage

This is where goat milk earns most of its fans. Its fat is packed into smaller globules, it forms a softer, looser curd in the stomach, and it usually contains less alpha-s1 casein, a protein some people find harder to digest in cow milk. Together, these may help it pass through more comfortably.

Many people who feel heavy or bloated after cow milk say goat milk sits better. Research comparing the two in adults is still limited, so treat it as something to test for yourself: a small glass with a meal is the simplest trial.

Lactose: a smaller difference than people expect

Goat milk has slightly less lactose than cow milk, but the gap is small. If you have true lactose intolerance, switching to goat milk is unlikely to solve it. Lactose-free cow milk is the more reliable option, and yoghurt, kefir and hard cheeses, from either animal, are often well tolerated because much of the lactose has already been broken down.

Allergies: no safe swap

This is where the choice matters most. A cow's milk allergy is an immune reaction to milk proteins, and goat milk proteins are very similar. A 1999 study in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology found that most children with cow's milk allergy also reacted to goat milk. If you or your child has a milk allergy, goat milk is not a safe alternative, and any change should be discussed with a doctor or dietitian.

Skin

Goat milk has the edge in skincare products. Its natural fats help hold moisture in the skin, and its lactic acid gently loosens dead skin cells, which is why goat milk soaps and lotions often feel creamier and less drying. When it comes to drinking either milk, there's no strong evidence that one gives you better skin than the other. Some studies have linked high intakes of milk, particularly skimmed cow milk, to acne in teenagers, so if your skin breaks out, cutting back is worth a try.

Gut and immune health

Both milks provide protein, zinc and vitamin A, which the immune system uses. Goat milk contains naturally occurring oligosaccharides, carbohydrates that may help feed beneficial gut bacteria, though research in adults is still early. If gut health is your main goal, fermented dairy from either animal, such as yoghurt and kefir, has stronger evidence behind it because it contains live cultures.

Price, taste and practicality

Cow milk is cheaper, easier to find and comes in many fat levels. Goat milk usually costs more, has a slightly tangy taste that some people love and others need to get used to, and is less likely to be fortified. If your budget is tight and cow milk agrees with you, there's no need to switch.

So which should you choose?

  • Choose goat milk if cow milk leaves you bloated or heavy, you like the taste, and the price works for you.
  • Choose cow milk if it agrees with you, you want more B12 and folate, or you need a lower-calorie option.
  • Choose lactose-free milk if you have true lactose intolerance.
  • Avoid both if you have a milk allergy, and speak to a professional about alternatives.

Whichever you choose, pick pasteurised milk, because raw milk from any animal can carry harmful bacteria, and don't give either as the main drink to babies under 12 months.

The bottom line

Goat milk and cow milk are closer than the marketing suggests. Goat milk is often easier to digest and slightly richer in a few minerals, while cow milk is cheaper, provides more folate and B12, and is easier to find in lighter versions. Neither is safe with a milk allergy, and neither fixes lactose intolerance on its own. The healthier choice is the one your body handles well.
