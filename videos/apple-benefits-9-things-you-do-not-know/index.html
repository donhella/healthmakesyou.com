Apple benefits - 9 things you do not know

The full guide

Most people think of an apple as a sweet, crunchy snack and nothing more. But there's a lot going on inside that simple fruit, from how it affects your blood sugar and cholesterol to how long it keeps you full. And one of the most useful parts is something many people throw away before the first bite. Here's what apples can do for you, and how to get the most from every one.

Apples and blood sugar

Apples taste sweet, so many people assume they're bad for blood sugar. In fact, they have a low glycaemic index, meaning their sugar is released into the blood fairly slowly. The fibre in an apple slows digestion, and natural plant compounds called polyphenols may also slow how quickly sugar is absorbed.

The research backs this up. A large study published in the BMJ in 2013, following nearly 190,000 adults, found that people who ate more whole fruits, including apples and pears, had a lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes. Fruit juice was a different story: drinking more of it was linked to a higher risk. So the whole fruit is what counts.

What apples do for your gut

A medium apple gives you around 4 grams of fibre, including a soluble fibre called pectin. Pectin acts as food for the friendly bacteria in your large intestine. As they feed on it, they produce short-chain fatty acids, which help keep the gut lining healthy. The fibre also helps keep you regular. If you're not used to much fibre, build up gradually and drink plenty of water to avoid bloating.

Apples and your heart

Here's a study worth knowing. In 2020, researchers published a trial in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition in which adults with mildly raised cholesterol ate two apples a day for eight weeks. Compared with a sugar-matched apple drink, the whole apples lowered their total and LDL cholesterol, the type linked to heart disease.

Pectin is thought to be part of the reason, because it helps the body remove cholesterol through the gut. The polyphenols in apples may also help keep blood vessels healthy.

Why you should keep the skin

This is the part many people throw away. A large share of an apple's fibre and an even bigger share of its antioxidants sit in or just under the skin. The peel contains plant compounds such as quercetin, which are found in much smaller amounts in the flesh. Peeling an apple removes a big chunk of what makes it so good for you.

Wash apples well under running water, rub them with your hands, and eat the skin. Our video on apple peel benefits goes into the peel in more detail.

Staying fuller for longer

Apples are full of water and fibre, and they take time to chew. All of that helps you feel satisfied. In a study published in the journal Appetite in 2009, people who ate apple slices before a lunch ate about 15% fewer calories at the meal than people who had nothing first. Apple sauce and apple juice didn't work nearly as well. Chewing whole fruit seems to tell your body you've eaten more.

Apples and weight management

Because they fill you up for very few calories, around 95 in a medium apple, apples can be a useful part of managing your weight. Swapping a biscuit or a bag of crisps for an apple saves calories and adds fibre at the same time. An apple won't melt fat on its own, but it makes it easier to eat a little less without feeling hungry.

The antioxidants you can't feel

You can't taste them, but apples are one of the richest everyday sources of polyphenols in many diets. These plant compounds act as antioxidants, helping protect your cells from damage. Research links diets rich in fruits and vegetables with a lower risk of heart disease and some cancers, and apples are an easy, cheap way to get more of these compounds every day.

Swapping out worse habits

One of the biggest benefits of an apple is what it replaces. An apple in your bag is an easy alternative to sweets, pastries or a fizzy drink when hunger hits. It's portable, needs no preparation and lasts for days. Small swaps like this add up over weeks and months.

Which apple is best?

The honest answer: the one you'll actually eat. All apple varieties provide fibre, vitamin C and polyphenols. Some research suggests that more colourful and tart varieties, like Granny Smith or red-skinned apples, may contain more polyphenols, but the difference matters far less than simply eating apples regularly, with the skin on.

A few tips:

  • Eat apples whole rather than as juice.
  • Keep the skin on and wash them well.
  • Pair an apple with a handful of nuts or a little peanut butter for a snack with protein.
  • One whole apple counts as one of your five a day.

The bottom line

Apples are a low-cost, everyday fruit that can help with steadier blood sugar, gut health and cholesterol, keep you full for few calories, and give you a steady supply of antioxidants. Most of the goodness is in or near the skin, so wash it and eat it. Choose whole apples over juice, and pick the variety you enjoy most.
