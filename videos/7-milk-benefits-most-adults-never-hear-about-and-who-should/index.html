7 milk benefits most adults never hear about (and who should avoid milk)

21 Nov 2026 · 9:25 · 7 min read

About this video

Milk benefits go far beyond strong bones in children. Here are 7 benefits of milk most adults never hear about, plus who should avoid it.

Milk can hydrate you better than water, help your muscles recover after exercise, protect your bones as you age, and supply iodine and vitamin B12 that many adults run short of. This video also explains how milk fits into the DASH approach for healthy blood pressure, why a glass of milk keeps you full longer than juice or a sugary drink, and whether full fat or low fat milk is the better choice for you.

We also look at what the research says about milk, acne and cancer, and who should avoid milk: people with a milk allergy, lactose intolerance, babies under 12 months, and anyone tempted by raw milk. You'll learn why lactose-free milk, yogurt, kefir and hard cheese are often easier on the stomach, and why goat milk is not a safe swap for a cow's milk allergy. Always talk to your doctor or dietitian before making big changes to your diet.

The full guide

Most of us were taught one thing about milk: it's good for your bones. That's true, but it's only a small part of the story. A plain glass of milk also helps keep you hydrated, feeds your muscles, supports your thyroid and energy levels, and can even help your blood pressure. Here are the seven benefits most adults never hear about, followed by the honest answers on full fat versus low fat, the scary headlines, and who should avoid milk altogether.

1. Milk can hydrate you better than water

It sounds wrong, but the research backs it up. In a 2016 study from Loughborough University in the UK, published in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, volunteers drank 13 different drinks and researchers measured how much of each one the body held on to over the next four hours. With water as the baseline, both full-fat and skimmed milk came out near the top, ahead of water, sports drinks, tea and coffee.

The reason is what milk contains. Natural salts such as sodium and potassium, along with protein, fat and natural sugar, slow down how quickly the fluid leaves your stomach and your body. Water is still your everyday drink, but after a hot day, a sweaty workout or a bout of illness, a glass of milk is a surprisingly good way to top up.

2. It helps your muscles recover

A 250 ml glass of milk gives you about 8 grams of protein, and it's a useful mix of two kinds. Whey is absorbed quickly, so it starts working on your muscles soon after you drink it. Casein is absorbed slowly, so it keeps feeding them for hours.

Studies on athletes have found that milk after exercise helps the body repair muscle and refill its energy stores, with plain or chocolate milk doing as well as many specialist recovery drinks. You don't need to be an athlete for this to matter. After carrying heavy shopping, gardening or a strength workout at home, a glass of milk with a meal gives your muscles the building blocks they need. That becomes more important with age, as we naturally start to lose muscle.

3. It supports your bones, but not on its own

Yes, milk gives you calcium. But bones also need phosphorus, protein, vitamin D and magnesium, and milk brings most of these together in one drink, in a form the body absorbs well.

Here's the honest part: milk alone won't stop your bones thinning. Bones get stronger when you put weight through them, by walking, climbing stairs and lifting things. Think of milk as the bricks and movement as the builder. People who drink milk but never move, and people who move but don't get enough calcium, both miss out.

4. It's a major source of iodine for your thyroid

This is the benefit almost nobody talks about. Your thyroid uses iodine to make the hormones that control your energy, weight and body temperature. In many countries, milk and dairy are one of the biggest sources of iodine in the diet. In the UK, they provide around a third of the iodine adults get, according to the British Dietetic Association.

Iodine matters even more in pregnancy, because a baby's developing brain depends on it. If you cut out milk without replacing that iodine with fish, eggs or iodised salt, you can slowly fall short without realising. Many plant milks contain little or no iodine unless it's added, so check the label.

5. It gives you vitamin B12 for energy

Your body needs vitamin B12 to make red blood cells, keep your nerves healthy and turn food into energy. When you run low, you can feel tired, foggy and weak, and you may get pins and needles in your hands and feet.

One glass of milk provides roughly half of the B12 an adult needs in a day. That's especially helpful if you eat little or no meat, and for older adults, because the body's ability to absorb B12 from food tends to fall with age. The NHS lists milk among the good everyday sources.

6. It can help your blood pressure

Milk is rich in potassium, calcium and magnesium, three minerals that help blood vessels relax and help the body get rid of extra salt.

In the well-known DASH trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1997, people who ate plenty of fruit and vegetables saw their blood pressure fall. But the group who ate fruit and vegetables plus low-fat dairy saw an even bigger drop. Milk won't replace blood pressure medication if you need it, but as part of a balanced, lower-salt diet, it's working on your side.

7. It keeps you full

The protein and fat in milk slow digestion, so you feel satisfied for longer than you would after juice or a fizzy drink with the same number of calories. Swapping a sugary drink for a glass of milk is one of the easiest changes you can make.

It works the other way too. For people who struggle to eat enough, such as older adults whose appetite has faded or anyone recovering from illness, a glass of milk is a simple way to get protein and nutrients in without facing a big plate of food.

Full fat or low fat?

For years we were told to choose skimmed milk to protect our hearts. More recent research is reassuring for whole milk. The large PURE study, published in The Lancet in 2018 and covering people in 21 countries, found that dairy intake, including full-fat dairy, was not linked to a higher risk of heart disease.

Full-fat milk does have more calories, so if you're watching your weight, semi-skimmed or skimmed is a sensible choice. If you prefer whole milk and it fits your diet, there's no need to feel guilty. The best milk is the one you'll actually drink, in sensible amounts.

What about acne and cancer?

Some studies have linked high milk intakes to acne in teenagers, particularly skimmed milk. If your skin seems to break out when you drink a lot of it, try cutting back and see what happens.

The research on cancer is mixed. Dairy is linked to a lower risk of bowel cancer, while some studies suggest very high intakes may be linked to prostate cancer in men. That's a good reason to enjoy milk in normal amounts, a glass or two a day, rather than litres of it.

Who should avoid milk?

  • People with a milk allergy. This is the immune system reacting to the proteins in milk, and it can cause hives, swelling, vomiting or breathing problems. It's different from lactose intolerance, and it means avoiding milk completely.
  • People with lactose intolerance, at least in larger amounts. Around two-thirds of adults worldwide lose some of their ability to digest lactose, the natural sugar in milk, and it's especially common in people of African, Asian and Latin American heritage. Signs include bloating, gas, cramps or loose stools a few hours after drinking milk. Many people can still manage a small amount with a meal, and often do well with yoghurt, kefir, hard cheese or lactose-free milk.
  • People with kidney disease, who should speak to their doctor first, because milk is high in phosphorus and potassium.
  • Babies under 12 months, who shouldn't have cow's milk as their main drink.

The goat milk mistake

Many people switch to goat milk thinking it solves every problem. Goat milk is easier on the stomach for some people, but it still contains lactose, so it won't fix true lactose intolerance for everyone. And if you have a cow's milk allergy, goat milk usually isn't safe either, because its proteins are very similar.

One more warning: raw, unpasteurised milk can carry germs such as E. coli, salmonella and listeria. The US Food and Drug Administration warns it's especially risky for pregnant women, young children, older adults and anyone with a weakened immune system.

The bottom line

Milk hydrates you well, helps your muscles recover, supports your bones when you pair it with movement, gives you iodine for your thyroid and B12 for your energy, helps with healthy blood pressure, and keeps you full. Full fat and low fat both have a place. Enjoy it in normal amounts. If milk doesn't agree with you, try lactose-free milk, yoghurt or hard cheese, and if you have a true milk allergy, avoid all animal milks, goat milk included.
