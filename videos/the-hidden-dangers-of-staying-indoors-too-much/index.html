The Hidden Dangers of Staying Indoors Too Much

21 Nov 2024 · 8:35

The full guide

Many of us now spend most of our lives indoors: at home, at work, in the car and back again. Staying inside can feel safe and cosy, but too much of it quietly affects your body and mind in ways that are easy to miss. Here's how limited sunlight and reduced movement affect your health, and simple ways to rebalance your indoor and outdoor time.

Limited sunlight and vitamin D

Your skin makes vitamin D when it's exposed to sunlight. Vitamin D helps your body absorb calcium and keeps bones, teeth and muscles healthy. Low levels can lead to aching bones, muscle weakness and, over time, conditions such as osteomalacia in adults, a softening of the bones.

If you rarely go outside, cover up most of your skin when you do, or have darker skin, you're at higher risk of low vitamin D. The NHS advises that everyone should consider a daily 10 microgram vitamin D supplement during autumn and winter, and that people who are indoors most of the time should consider one all year round.

Light, your body clock and sleep

Daylight is the main signal that sets your internal body clock. Bright light in the morning tells your brain it's daytime, helping you feel alert, and sets you up to feel sleepy at the right time at night. Indoor lighting is far dimmer than daylight, even on a cloudy day.

Without enough daylight, your body clock can drift, which can lead to poorer sleep, low energy during the day and a later bedtime. Getting outside in the morning, even for 10 to 20 minutes, is one of the simplest ways to sleep better.

Mood and mental health

Light also affects mood. Some people develop seasonal affective disorder (SAD), a type of depression that comes and goes with the seasons, usually worse in the darker months when there's less daylight. Many more people notice lower mood and energy when they spend long stretches indoors.

Time in green spaces seems to help. A 2019 study in Scientific Reports, covering nearly 20,000 people in England, found that those who spent at least 120 minutes a week in nature were significantly more likely to report good health and wellbeing than those who spent no time there. It didn't matter whether the time came in one long visit or several short ones.

The risks of reduced physical activity

Staying indoors usually means sitting more and moving less. The World Health Organization describes physical inactivity as one of the leading risk factors for death from non-communicable diseases, raising the risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, some cancers and depression.

Long periods of sitting also weaken muscles, stiffen joints and slow your metabolism. Over the years, that can affect your balance, strength and independence. The NHS recommends adults do at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, plus strength exercises on two days, and break up long periods of sitting.

Indoor air and eyes

Indoor air can contain more pollutants than you'd expect, from cooking, cleaning products, damp and mould. Opening windows regularly and using extractor fans helps. And for children especially, spending more time outdoors has been linked with a lower risk of developing short-sightedness, likely because of daylight and looking at things in the distance.

Social connection

Spending most of your time inside can also mean seeing fewer people. Loneliness and social isolation are linked to poorer physical and mental health, especially in older adults. Going outside, even for a short walk, creates chances to chat with neighbours, join a group or simply feel part of the world.

Tips to balance indoor and outdoor time

  • Start the day with daylight. Have your morning drink by an open window or step outside for 10 minutes.
  • Walk where you can. Take calls on a walk, get off the bus a stop early, or walk after meals.
  • Find green space. Parks, gardens and tree-lined streets all count. Aim for about two hours a week in total.
  • Move indoors too. If you can't get out, stand up every 30 to 45 minutes, do some stretches or follow an exercise video.
  • Work near a window to get more natural light during the day.
  • Air your home. Open windows daily, even briefly in winter.
  • Consider vitamin D in autumn and winter, or all year if you're mostly indoors.
  • Make it social. Walk with a friend or join a local group.

Protect your skin in strong sun by seeking shade around midday and using sunscreen, especially in summer, and don't let your skin burn.

The bottom line

Staying indoors too much can lower your vitamin D, upset your body clock and sleep, dampen your mood, reduce your activity and cut you off from others. The fix doesn't need to be dramatic: a little morning daylight, regular walks, time in green spaces and breaking up long periods of sitting all add up. Your body was built to spend time outside, so give it some every day.
