Sourdough vs normal bread. What's the real difference?

The full guide

Sourdough and regular bread can look almost identical on the shelf, but the way they're made creates some real differences in taste, texture and how your body handles them. Here's what actually separates sourdough from normal bread, why fermentation matters, what it means for blood sugar and digestion, and what to weigh up before you choose your next loaf.

How each bread is made

Most everyday bread is made with commercial baker's yeast. The dough is mixed, rises quickly, often in an hour or two, and is baked. In factory loaves, the process can be even faster, helped along by additives that strengthen the dough and keep it soft for longer.

Real sourdough is made with a "starter": a mix of flour and water that has been left to ferment so it's full of wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria. A portion of starter is added to the dough, which then ferments slowly, often for 12 to 48 hours, before baking. That long, slow fermentation is what makes sourdough different.

Why fermentation changes things

During the long fermentation, the bacteria in the starter produce lactic and acetic acids. These give sourdough its tangy taste and chewy crust, and they change the dough in other ways:

  • They partly break down starches and proteins in the flour.
  • They reduce phytic acid, a compound in grains that can bind minerals like iron, zinc and magnesium, so more of these may be available to your body.
  • They help the bread stay fresh for longer without added preservatives.

What about blood sugar?

Some studies have found that sourdough bread causes a smaller rise in blood sugar than the same type of bread made with ordinary yeast, likely because the acids slow down how quickly starch is digested. The effect varies, though, and depends a lot on the flour. A white sourdough is still made from refined white flour and still raises blood sugar.

If blood sugar is your concern, the type of flour matters as much as the method. Wholegrain or rye sourdough, eaten alongside protein, vegetables and healthy fats, is the best choice. Our guide to eating in the right order to stop blood sugar spikes explains how to pair bread with other foods.

Digestion and gluten

Many people find sourdough easier on their stomach. One reason is that long fermentation breaks down some of the fructans in wheat, a type of FODMAP: carbohydrates that can cause bloating and wind in people with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). Research suggests traditional sourdough contains lower levels of these than quick yeasted bread.

The important caveat: sourdough made from wheat, rye or barley still contains gluten. It is not safe for people with coeliac disease, no matter how long it's fermented. If you have coeliac disease, you need certified gluten-free bread.

Taste and texture differences

Sourdough has a deeper, slightly sour flavour, a chewy, open crumb and a thick, crackly crust. Regular sandwich bread is softer, milder and more uniform. Neither is "right"; it's a matter of what you enjoy. Sourdough tends to be best toasted or with savoury toppings, while soft bread suits children's sandwiches and anyone who finds a hard crust difficult to chew.

Cost, convenience and what to consider

Real sourdough usually costs more because it takes much longer to make. It can also go stale faster than factory bread full of preservatives, though it keeps well when sliced and frozen.

Be aware that some shop-bought loaves labelled "sourdough" are made quickly with yeast and a bit of sourdough flavouring, so they don't carry the same benefits. Check the ingredients: real sourdough usually lists only flour, water and salt, with no added yeast. Our video on how to spot fake sourdough goes into this in detail.

When choosing, think about:

  • Flour: wholegrain or rye gives more fibre than white, whichever method is used.
  • Ingredients: short lists are a good sign.
  • Your gut: if regular bread bloats you, real sourdough is worth trying.
  • Budget: a good wholemeal loaf made with yeast is still a healthy choice.

The bottom line

The real difference between sourdough and normal bread is fermentation. Long, slow sourdough fermentation creates a tangy, chewy loaf that may be easier to digest for some people, may raise blood sugar a little less, and may make some minerals easier to absorb. But it still contains gluten, white sourdough is still refined bread, and not every loaf labelled "sourdough" is the real thing. Choose real, preferably wholegrain sourdough if you enjoy it, and enjoy it as part of a balanced meal.
