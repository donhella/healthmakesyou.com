Why menopause joint pain flares up (and how to calm it)

The full guide

Your heels hurt with the very first step out of bed. Your fingers feel stiff and swollen until mid-morning. One shoulder has become so painful you can't reach behind your back, and you can't remember hurting it. When you finally see a doctor, you're told it's your age, or an X-ray shows nothing. If that sounds familiar, your hormones may well be part of the story. Here's why menopause joint pain flares up, which joints it usually hits, the signs that point to something else, and the practical steps that calm it, with or without hormone therapy.

How common it is

In 2024, orthopaedic surgeon Dr Vonda Wright and colleagues published a paper in the journal Climacteric that gave this a name: the musculoskeletal syndrome of menopause. They reported that around 70% of women going through the menopause transition have muscle and joint pain, and about a quarter will be disabled by it at some point.

That isn't a small, unlucky group. It's most women. Yet conversations about menopause often stop at hot flushes and mood swings, which is why so many women feel blindsided when their joints start to ache.

Why it happens: oestrogen does more than you think

Oestrogen isn't only a reproductive hormone. There are oestrogen receptors in your cartilage, joint linings, tendons, ligaments, muscles and bones. Oestrogen helps your body make collagen, the protein that keeps tendons and ligaments strong but flexible. It helps joints move smoothly and has a calming effect on inflammation.

Around menopause, oestrogen swings up and down, sometimes wildly, before settling much lower. As it falls:

  • Collagen production slows, so tissues get stiffer and slower to repair
  • Inflammation becomes easier to switch on
  • Muscle mass starts to slip away, so joints carry more load
  • Night sweats break up sleep, which lowers how much pain anyone can tolerate

It also explains why women who have surgical menopause, when the ovaries are removed, often notice joint pain suddenly. Their oestrogen drop happens almost overnight.

Where it shows up

  • Hands: stiff, achy fingers first thing in the morning
  • Hips: especially at night when lying on one side
  • Knees: on the stairs
  • Neck
  • Feet: plantar fasciitis that makes the first steps of the day feel like walking on glass, or a sore Achilles tendon

The shoulder deserves special mention. Frozen shoulder is most common between 40 and 60 and affects women more than men. It can last for years, and sometimes moves to the other shoulder once the first one releases.

"Flare" is the right word, because the pain often comes and goes. It's usually worst at night and first thing in the morning, and tends to worsen after poor sleep, a stressful week, a sudden burst of activity or a few days of sitting still.

When it's something else

Not every ache after 50 is menopause, and you deserve a proper check rather than a shrug.

  • Swollen, warm, soft knuckles and wrists on both sides, with morning stiffness lasting more than about an hour: this can be rheumatoid arthritis, and early treatment protects the joints.
  • Over 50, sudden pain and stiffness in both shoulders and both hips, struggling to lift your arms in the morning, unusual tiredness: ask about polymyalgia rheumatica. It's often missed, is diagnosed with simple blood tests and usually responds well to treatment.
  • A single hot, red, swollen joint, especially with a fever: be seen the same day.
  • Pain after a fall when you know your bones are thin: get it checked.

It's also worth asking for your thyroid and vitamin D levels, because both can cause aches that look like menopause. If you're told it's just your age without any further look, it's reasonable to ask for a second opinion.

What about hormone therapy?

For many women, hormone replacement therapy (HRT) makes a real difference. In the large Women's Health Initiative study, women taking oestrogen reported joint pain less often than women taking a dummy pill. Research presented to the North American Menopause Society in 2022 also found women on hormone therapy were less likely to be diagnosed with frozen shoulder.

But it isn't a magic switch for everyone. Some women find it helps without taking everything away, and many can't take it, for example after certain breast cancers, with some autoimmune conditions or with a history of blood clots. Women taking breast cancer tablets called aromatase inhibitors often get joint pain from the medicine itself. Whether HRT is right for you is a conversation for a doctor, ideally one with menopause training, who knows your full history.

What helps, with or without hormones

  • Strength training. The strongest evidence. Muscle protects joints and slows the muscle loss that comes with falling oestrogen. Start light, work the big muscle groups two or three times a week, and build slowly.
  • Daily movement. Walking keeps joints lubricated, and stiff joints usually feel better once warm. For a stiff shoulder, gentle exercise within your pain limits, ideally guided by a physiotherapist, beats holding it still.
  • Protein at every meal. Eggs, fish, beans, lentils, yoghurt or chicken help you keep and rebuild muscle; many women in midlife don't eat enough.
  • A Mediterranean style of eating. Plenty of vegetables, olive oil, beans and oily fish like sardines and mackerel, with less sugar and processed food, is linked to lower inflammation.
  • Check your vitamin D. Low levels can add to aches and weaken bones.
  • Ease the load on your knees. Research from Wake Forest University found that every pound of weight lost takes about four pounds of pressure off the knees with each step.
  • Warmth and good shoes. A warm shower or heat pad eases morning stiffness; cushioned, supportive shoes help painful heels.
  • Protect your sleep. Treating night sweats and building a calm bedtime routine can lower the pain you feel the next day.

The bottom line

Menopause joint pain is real, common and has a clear cause: falling oestrogen affects cartilage, tendons, muscles and inflammation. It tends to hit the hands, shoulders, hips, knees and heels, and flares at night, in the morning and after stress or poor sleep. Watch for the signs that point elsewhere. HRT helps many women, but not all, and not everyone can take it. Whatever you decide, strength training, daily movement, enough protein, healthy vitamin D, good sleep and steady weight management calm the pain and protect your joints. You're not imagining it, and you're not just getting old.
