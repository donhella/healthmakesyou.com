Nutrition for seniors - 10 foods that add years to your life

The full guide

Good nutrition after 60 doesn't have to mean expensive supplements or complicated diets. Many of the foods that help most are cheap, familiar and probably already in your kitchen. As we age, our bodies need more protein to hold on to muscle, more fibre for digestion and the heart, and nutrients that support the brain and bones. Here are ten everyday foods that may help add healthy years to your life, and how to make the most of each.

1. Eggs

Eggs are one of the easiest sources of high-quality protein there is: cheap, quick to cook and soft enough for anyone with chewing difficulties. One egg gives you about 6 grams of protein, plus vitamin B12, vitamin D, choline and the eye-protecting nutrients lutein and zeaxanthin.

Protein matters more with age because the body loses muscle faster and uses protein less efficiently. Boiled, scrambled or in an omelette with vegetables, eggs make it easy to start the day with a solid dose.

2. Greek yoghurt

Plain Greek yoghurt is thick, creamy and much higher in protein than regular yoghurt, around 13 to 15 grams in a typical pot. It also provides calcium for bones and, in many brands, live cultures for gut health. Its biggest advantage for older adults is convenience: no cooking, easy to eat and ready in seconds. Choose plain, unsweetened yoghurt and add your own fruit.

3. Sardines

This tiny fish has big benefits. Sardines are rich in omega-3 fats, which support the heart and brain, and because you eat the soft bones, they're also an excellent source of calcium. They provide vitamin D and B12 too, both of which older adults often run short of. The NHS recommends at least two portions of fish a week, one of them oily. Tinned sardines on wholegrain toast is one of the cheapest, easiest ways to get there.

4. Beans and lentils

Beans, chickpeas and lentils give you protein and fibre in the same spoonful, plus folate, potassium and magnesium. Fibre helps keep you regular, a common problem in later life, supports healthy cholesterol and feeds the good bacteria in your gut. They're cheap, keep for ages in tins and fit easily into soups, stews and salads. If they give you wind, start with small portions and build up.

5. Leafy greens

Spinach, kale, cabbage and other leafy greens become even more valuable with age. They provide vitamin K, folate, lutein and other compounds linked to brain health. A study published in Neurology in 2018 found that older adults who ate about one serving of leafy greens a day had slower cognitive decline than those who rarely ate them.

One note: if you take warfarin, keep your intake of leafy greens steady rather than suddenly eating much more or less, because their vitamin K affects how the medicine works.

6. Berries

Blueberries, strawberries and raspberries are rich in fibre, vitamin C and colourful plant compounds called anthocyanins, which may support brain and heart health. They're not a miracle food, and no fruit can prevent memory loss on its own, but they're a delicious way to add nutrition and natural sweetness. Frozen berries are just as nutritious, cheaper and last for months.

7. Nuts

A small handful of nuts a day has been linked to a lower risk of heart disease and a longer life in large studies. Nuts provide healthy fats, protein, fibre, vitamin E and magnesium. The key is portion size: nuts are calorie-dense, so about 30 grams, a small handful, is plenty. Choose unsalted varieties, and if chewing is difficult, try nut butters spread thinly.

8. Oats

A bowl of porridge is warm, comforting and genuinely good for you. Oats contain beta-glucan, a soluble fibre that helps lower cholesterol and slows the rise in blood sugar after eating. To build a better bowl, skip the sugary instant sachets and use plain oats, then add berries, a spoon of nuts or seeds, and some milk or Greek yoghurt for protein.

9. Avocado

Avocado is high in fat, but it's the right kind: mostly monounsaturated fat, which helps keep cholesterol healthy when it replaces saturated fat. It also provides fibre, potassium and vitamin E, and its soft texture makes it easy to eat. Half an avocado on toast or in a salad is a satisfying portion.

10. Whole grains

Wholemeal bread, brown rice, wholewheat pasta and other whole grains keep the fibre-rich outer layers that refined grains lose. Diets rich in whole grains are linked to lower risks of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and some cancers. Swapping white for wholegrain versions of the foods you already eat is one of the simplest upgrades you can make.

The bigger message

No single food adds years to your life. It's the overall pattern that matters: plenty of protein spread through the day, lots of fibre from beans, oats, whole grains and vegetables, healthy fats from fish, nuts and avocado, and colour from greens and berries. Combine that with staying active, drinking enough fluids and keeping in touch with others, and you give your body the best chance of staying strong and independent for longer.

If your appetite is small, focus on nutrient-dense foods like eggs, yoghurt, nuts and sardines, and consider smaller, more frequent meals. And if you've lost weight without trying, speak to your GP. For a second list of ideas, see Best foods for seniors.
