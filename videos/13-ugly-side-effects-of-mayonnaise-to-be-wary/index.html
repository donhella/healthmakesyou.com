13 Ugly Side Effects of Mayonnaise to Be Wary Of

The full guide

Mayonnaise turns a plain sandwich into something special, and it's a staple in salads, dips and burgers. But it's one of the most calorie-dense foods in the fridge, and it's easy to use far more than you think. Here are the side effects of eating too much mayonnaise, from weight and cholesterol to food safety and allergies, plus a fair look at which worries are overblown.

Weight gain

Mayonnaise is mostly oil, which makes it very high in calories. A single tablespoon of regular mayonnaise has around 90 to 100 calories, and a generous dollop on a sandwich or in a potato salad can easily be three or four tablespoons. That can add 300 to 400 calories to a meal without making you feel any fuller. Over weeks and months, extra calories like these can lead to weight gain.

Cholesterol and heart health

The fat in most mayonnaise comes from vegetable oils such as soybean, sunflower or rapeseed oil, so it's mainly unsaturated fat rather than the saturated fat most linked to high cholesterol. That's better than some people assume.

The bigger issue is where mayonnaise ends up: in fried chicken sandwiches, burgers, creamy coleslaw and loaded fries. Those meals tend to be high in saturated fat, salt and calories, which together can raise cholesterol and blood pressure over time.

Stroke and blood pressure

Mayonnaise itself doesn't cause strokes. But a diet heavy in salty, high-calorie processed foods raises the risk of high blood pressure and weight gain, two of the biggest risk factors for stroke and heart disease. Mayo-heavy fast food is often part of that pattern, so cutting back is a sensible step for your blood vessels.

Food poisoning from homemade mayo

This is one of the most real risks. Homemade mayonnaise is often made with raw egg yolks, which can carry salmonella. Salmonella food poisoning causes diarrhoea, stomach cramps, vomiting and fever. Shop-bought mayonnaise is made with pasteurised eggs and is safe when stored properly.

To stay safe:

  • Use pasteurised eggs if you make your own, especially for pregnant women, young children and older adults.
  • Refrigerate opened jars and use them within the time on the label.
  • Don't leave mayo-based salads or sandwiches out in the heat for more than a couple of hours.

Digestive discomfort

Because it's so high in fat, a large amount of mayonnaise can be heavy on the stomach. Fat slows digestion, which can lead to bloating, nausea or discomfort, especially for people with gallbladder problems or a sensitive gut.

Egg allergy

Mayonnaise contains egg, one of the most common food allergens, especially in children. Reactions can range from hives and itching to swelling and breathing problems. Anyone with an egg allergy needs to avoid mayonnaise and check labels on sauces, dressings and salads, where it's often hidden. Egg-free versions are available.

Skin problems

You'll often read that mayonnaise causes breakouts. There's no good evidence that mayonnaise itself causes acne. What we can say is that diets high in processed, fried and sugary foods, which mayo often accompanies, have been linked with poorer skin in some studies, so a generally balanced diet is the better target.

Mental health and mood

Again, there's no evidence that mayonnaise harms mental health. Large reviews have linked diets high in ultra-processed foods with a higher risk of depression and other health problems, though cause and effect isn't proven. Many shop-bought mayonnaises fall into that processed category, so it's another reason to treat it as a condiment, not a main ingredient.

Hidden salt and sugar

Many mayonnaises and mayo-based sauces contain added salt and sometimes sugar. Light versions, in particular, may add sugar or starch to make up for the reduced fat. Check labels and compare.

How to enjoy mayonnaise more healthily

  • Measure it. One level tablespoon is a portion.
  • Spread thinly. A thin layer gives the flavour with fewer calories.
  • Mix it. Combine half mayo with half plain Greek yoghurt for dips and coleslaw.
  • Try alternatives. Mashed avocado, hummus or mustard work well in sandwiches.
  • Choose lighter versions if you use it often, but check the sugar.

The bottom line

Mayonnaise isn't poison, and its fat is mostly the unsaturated kind. The real side effects come from large portions and what it's eaten with: extra calories, heavy fast-food meals, salt, and digestive discomfort. Homemade versions with raw eggs carry a food poisoning risk, and people with an egg allergy must avoid it. Use a level tablespoon, mix it with yoghurt, and keep it as a condiment.
