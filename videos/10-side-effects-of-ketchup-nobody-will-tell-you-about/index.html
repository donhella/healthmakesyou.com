10 Side Effects of Ketchup Nobody Will Tell You About

The full guide

Ketchup is on almost every table, and a squeeze here and there isn't going to hurt you. But because it's so easy to pour on everything, many people eat far more of it than they realise. Here are ten side effects of eating too much ketchup, what's really in the bottle, and how to enjoy it without overdoing it.

1. More sugar than you'd expect

Ketchup is sweeter than most people think. Standard ketchup contains roughly a teaspoon of sugar in every tablespoon, and it's often one of the main ingredients after tomatoes. A few generous squirts on chips, a burger and a sausage can easily add up to several teaspoons of sugar in one meal.

The NHS advises adults to have no more than 30 grams of free sugars a day, about seven sugar cubes. Ketchup alone won't take you over that, but alongside fizzy drinks, sauces, cereals and snacks, it quietly adds to the total.

2. Hidden sugar in a "savoury" food

Because ketchup tastes savoury, it rarely registers as a sugary food. That's what makes it easy to overeat. Many people who carefully avoid sweets don't think twice about ketchup, barbecue sauce or sweet chilli sauce, which can all be high in sugar.

3. A sodium surprise

Ketchup is also salty. A tablespoon contains around 150 to 200 mg of sodium. That might not sound like much, but adults are advised to have no more than 6 grams of salt a day, and most people already eat more than that. Too much salt raises blood pressure, which increases the risk of heart disease and stroke.

4. It pairs with the saltiest foods

The bigger issue is what ketchup goes on. Chips, burgers, hot dogs, sausages and fried snacks are already high in salt, fat and calories. Ketchup makes these foods more enjoyable, which can mean eating more of them, more often.

5. Heartburn and acid reflux

Tomatoes are naturally acidic, and ketchup adds vinegar on top. For people with acid reflux or heartburn, tomato-based foods are a common trigger. If you often get a burning feeling in your chest after meals, try cutting back on ketchup and tomato sauces to see if it helps.

6. Tough on your teeth

The combination of sugar and acid isn't great for tooth enamel. Sugar feeds the bacteria that cause tooth decay, and acid can soften enamel. Rinsing with water after eating and waiting before brushing helps protect your teeth.

7. The preservative problem

Many ketchups contain preservatives such as sodium benzoate to keep them fresh. These are approved as safe in normal amounts. A small number of people are sensitive to benzoates and may get hives, itching or other reactions. If you notice symptoms after foods containing them, check labels and speak to a doctor.

8. Weight gain over time

A single serving of ketchup is low in calories, but frequent, generous use adds sugar and calories that are easy to overlook. More importantly, ketchup tends to come with fried, calorie-dense foods. Over months, those habits can contribute to weight gain.

9. Blood sugar spikes

Because ketchup is high in sugar and low in fibre, large amounts can contribute to a rise in blood sugar, especially when eaten with refined carbs like white bread buns and chips. For people with diabetes or prediabetes, it's worth measuring portions rather than pouring freely.

10. It crowds out real tomatoes

Ketchup does contain lycopene, an antioxidant found in tomatoes, and cooked tomato products are actually a good source of it. But ketchup isn't a vegetable serving. Relying on it for your "tomato" can mean missing out on whole tomatoes, which give you lycopene with fibre, vitamin C and potassium and without the added sugar and salt.

Who should be most careful?

  • People with high blood pressure, who need to watch their salt.
  • People with diabetes or prediabetes, because of the sugar.
  • Anyone with acid reflux or heartburn.
  • Children, who often eat ketchup in large amounts and are more affected by added sugar.

How to enjoy ketchup more healthily

  • Measure it. A tablespoon on the side of your plate, not a flood over the top.
  • Choose reduced-sugar and reduced-salt versions. Compare labels; the differences can be big.
  • Dip, don't drown. Dipping uses less than pouring.
  • Make your own. Simmer tomato purée with a little vinegar, onion, garlic and spices, and add only a small amount of sweetener.
  • Try other toppings. Fresh salsa, sliced tomatoes, mustard or a yoghurt dip can add flavour with less sugar.

The bottom line

Ketchup is fine in small amounts, and it even contains some lycopene. The side effects come from how much and how often we use it: added sugar, salt, acidity and its habit of travelling with fried foods. Measure your portions, choose lower-sugar and lower-salt versions, and let fresh tomatoes do the heavy lifting.
