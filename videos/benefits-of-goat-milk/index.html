Benefits of Goat Milk

2 Jul 2020 · 4:28

The full guide

Goat milk is the most widely drunk milk in much of the world, yet many people have never tried it. It has a creamy, slightly tangy taste, and it differs from cow milk in a few ways that can make it easier on some stomachs. Here's what goat milk gives you, why some people find it gentler, where it falls short, and the other ways people use it beyond the glass.

What's in a glass of goat milk?

A cup of goat milk (about 245 ml) is roughly as nourishing as cow milk, with around 8 to 9 grams of protein plus calcium, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium and vitamin A. Goats convert the beta-carotene in their feed into vitamin A before it reaches the milk, which is why goat milk is so white.

It tends to be a little richer than cow milk, with slightly more fat and calories per cup, and a bit more calcium and potassium. It also has its weaker spots, which we'll come to: it's lower in folate and vitamin B12 than cow milk.

Why some people find it easier to digest

This is the benefit goat milk is best known for, and there are a few reasons behind it.

  • Smaller fat globules. The fat in goat milk is packed into smaller droplets, giving the digestive enzymes more surface to work on.
  • More medium-chain fats. Goat milk is richer in medium-chain fatty acids such as caprylic and capric acid. The body absorbs these quickly and uses them for energy rather than storing them as readily.
  • A softer curd. In the stomach, goat milk forms a softer, looser curd than cow milk, which may pass through more easily.
  • A different protein mix. Goat milk generally contains less of a casein protein called alpha-s1 casein, one of the proteins some people react to in cow milk.

None of this makes goat milk magic, and studies comparing the two in adults are still limited. But it does explain why many people who feel heavy or bloated after cow milk say goat milk sits better with them.

A little less lactose, but not lactose-free

Goat milk contains slightly less lactose than cow milk, the natural sugar that causes bloating, gas and cramps in people who are lactose intolerant. The difference is small, though, so it isn't a fix for true lactose intolerance.

If you're mildly sensitive, a small glass of goat milk with a meal may be easier to handle. If you're properly intolerant, the NHS suggests options such as lactose-free milk, or foods that contain less lactose, like hard cheese and yoghurt. Goat milk yoghurt and kefir can also work well, because the fermentation has already broken down some of the lactose.

Not a safe swap for a milk allergy

This is the most important point to get right. A cow's milk allergy is the immune system reacting to milk proteins, and goat milk proteins are very similar. A study in children with cow's milk allergy, published in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology in 1999, found that the large majority also reacted to goat milk.

So if you or your child has a diagnosed milk allergy, goat milk is not a safe alternative. And goat milk shouldn't be given to babies under 12 months as their main drink. Infant formulas based on goat milk exist and are made to meet the same standards as other formulas; plain goat milk from a carton is not.

Vitamins and minerals worth knowing about

Goat milk is a good source of several nutrients your body uses every day:

  • Calcium and phosphorus for bones and teeth.
  • Potassium, which helps balance the effects of salt on blood pressure.
  • Vitamin A for vision, skin and the immune system.
  • Riboflavin (vitamin B2), which helps turn food into energy.

The gaps matter too. Goat milk is naturally low in folate and lower in vitamin B12 than cow milk, so if it becomes your main milk, make sure you're getting those from other foods such as leafy greens, beans, eggs, fish and meat.

More than a drink

Goat milk is also used to make soft and hard cheeses (including many types of feta and chèvre), yoghurt, kefir, butter, and skincare products such as soap and lotion. Goat milk soap is popular with people who have dry skin, because the milk fats and lactic acid make it creamy and gentle. If skincare is your interest, our goat milk soap video goes into it in detail.

How to try it

Start with a small glass with a meal and see how your stomach feels. Try it in porridge, smoothies or tea, where the slightly tangy taste blends in. Always choose pasteurised goat milk: raw milk, from goats or cows, can carry harmful bacteria such as E. coli, salmonella and listeria, and is especially risky for pregnant women, young children and older adults.

The bottom line

Goat milk is a nutritious, creamy milk that many people find easier to digest than cow milk, thanks to its smaller fat globules, softer curd and different protein mix. It's rich in calcium, potassium and vitamin A, but lower in folate and B12. It has only slightly less lactose, and it isn't safe for people with a cow's milk allergy. Choose pasteurised, start small, and see how your body responds.
