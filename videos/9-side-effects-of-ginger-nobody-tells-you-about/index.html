9 Side Effects of Ginger Nobody Tells You About

The full guide

Ginger is one of the most loved natural remedies in the world, used for nausea, colds, sore muscles and digestion. In normal food amounts it's safe for most people. But because ginger is so powerful, taking a lot of it, especially as shots, strong teas, capsules or powders, can cause problems some people never expect. Here are nine side effects worth knowing about, and who should be most careful.

1. Low blood sugar

Several small studies suggest ginger may help lower blood sugar. That sounds like good news, and for some people it is. But if you have diabetes and take medicine that lowers blood sugar, adding large amounts of ginger on top could push your levels too low. Signs include shakiness, sweating, dizziness and confusion. If you take diabetes medication, speak to your doctor before using ginger supplements or daily ginger shots.

2. Diarrhoea

Ginger speeds up the movement of food through your digestive system. That's part of why it helps with bloating and nausea, but in large amounts it can go too far and cause loose stools or diarrhoea. If you notice this after ginger shots or very strong ginger tea, cut back.

3. Stomach discomfort

It surprises many people, but the same root that settles one stomach can upset another. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health lists abdominal discomfort among ginger's most common side effects, along with irritation of the mouth and throat. Raw ginger on an empty stomach is the most likely culprit, so try taking it with food.

4. Low blood pressure

Some research suggests ginger may help relax blood vessels and lower blood pressure slightly. For most people that's harmless, but if you already have low blood pressure, or you take medicine to lower it, large amounts of ginger could leave you feeling light-headed or faint. Again, normal food amounts are rarely an issue; the concern is concentrated doses.

5. Heartburn and acid reflux

Ginger is often recommended for indigestion, yet heartburn is one of its most commonly reported side effects. Strong ginger can irritate the lining of the food pipe, especially in people who already have reflux. If ginger tea leaves you with a burning feeling in your chest, switch to milder amounts or stop.

6. Increased bleeding risk

Ginger may slightly reduce the blood's ability to clot. On its own, in food amounts, this is unlikely to matter. But if you take blood thinners such as warfarin, aspirin or clopidogrel, or you have a bleeding disorder, large amounts of ginger could add to the effect. It's also sensible to stop ginger supplements about two weeks before planned surgery, and to tell your surgeon if you take them.

7. Allergic reactions

Ginger allergy is uncommon, but it does happen. Symptoms can include a rash, itching, a tingling mouth or, rarely, swelling and difficulty breathing. If you notice any reaction after eating ginger, stop and speak to a doctor. Severe swelling or breathing problems need urgent medical help.

8. Interactions with medicines

Because ginger can affect blood sugar, blood pressure and blood clotting, it can interact with medicines that do the same things. The main groups to watch are:

  • Blood thinners and anti-platelet medicines
  • Diabetes medicines
  • Blood pressure medicines

If you take any regular medication, check with your pharmacist or doctor before adding ginger supplements, ginger shots or large daily amounts.

9. Kidney stones

Ginger contains oxalate, a natural compound that can combine with calcium to form the most common type of kidney stone. Eating ginger in normal amounts won't cause stones, but if you've had calcium oxalate stones before, it's wise to avoid very high intakes of ginger powder and supplements, and to drink plenty of water.

How much ginger is safe?

For most healthy adults, ginger in cooking, a cup or two of ginger tea, or a few slices in hot water is perfectly fine. Problems usually come from concentrated forms: shots, capsules, powders taken by the spoonful, or several strong drinks a day. A common guide is to stay under about 4 grams of ginger a day, which is roughly a thumb-sized piece of fresh root.

Who should be careful?

  • Pregnant women: ginger is widely used for morning sickness, and research suggests it may help. Stick to modest amounts and talk to your midwife or doctor before taking supplements.
  • People on blood thinners, diabetes or blood pressure medicine.
  • Anyone with gallstones, since ginger can increase the flow of bile.
  • People with a history of kidney stones.
  • Anyone having surgery soon.

The bottom line

Ginger is a healthy, useful spice, and most people can enjoy it every day without problems. The side effects, from diarrhoea and heartburn to low blood sugar and bleeding risk, mostly appear with large or concentrated amounts, or when ginger is combined with certain medicines. Enjoy it in food and tea, go easy on shots and supplements, and check with your doctor or pharmacist if you take regular medication.
