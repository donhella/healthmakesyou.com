The full guide

"Sourdough" on the front of a bag doesn't always mean you're getting real sourdough. Many loaves are made quickly with ordinary yeast and a little sourdough flavour, then sold at a premium price. Campaigners even have a name for it: "sourfaux". Here's how to tell the real thing from bread that's only marketed that way, what to check on the label and in the loaf, and what to ask at the bakery.

Why fermentation time matters

Real sourdough rises using a starter, a living mix of flour and water full of wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria. The dough ferments slowly, typically for 12 hours or more, sometimes up to two days. That long fermentation is where sourdough's flavour, texture and possible health benefits come from.

Fake sourdough skips the wait. It's made with commercial yeast so it rises in an hour or two, then gets a sour taste from added acids, vinegar or a dried sourdough powder. It may look similar, but it hasn't been through the process that makes sourdough different.

What fermentation changes

During a long, slow ferment, the bacteria in the starter:

  • Produce lactic and acetic acids, which give the tang and help the bread keep without preservatives
  • Break down some of the fructans in wheat, carbohydrates that can cause bloating in people with irritable bowel syndrome
  • Reduce phytic acid, which may make minerals like iron and zinc easier to absorb
  • May slow how quickly the starch is digested, softening the blood sugar rise

A quick yeasted loaf with sourdough flavouring doesn't get these changes, or gets far less of them.

What real sourdough should taste like

Real sourdough has a complex, gently sour flavour that develops as you chew, with a deep, caramelised crust. The tang should feel natural, not sharp or vinegary. A loaf that tastes flat and sweet underneath a sour top note, or has a harsh vinegar edge, may have had acid added rather than developed.

Ingredients worth checking

This is the most reliable test. Real sourdough needs only three ingredients: flour, water and salt, with the starter (sometimes listed as "sourdough culture" or "fermented wheat flour").

Watch out for:

  • Yeast listed as an ingredient, a strong sign of a quick-rise loaf
  • Vinegar, lactic acid or acetic acid added for flavour
  • "Sourdough powder" or "dried sourdough"
  • Preservatives, emulsifiers or flour treatment agents, which are rarely needed in real sourdough
  • Sugar or fat, which aren't part of traditional sourdough

What the bread itself can reveal

  • Crust: real sourdough usually has a thick, dark, crackly crust.
  • Crumb: often irregular, with holes of different sizes, and a slightly glossy, chewy texture.
  • Softness: factory "sourdough" is often very soft, uniform and sliced like sandwich bread.
  • Shelf life: real sourdough stays fresh for a few days but goes stale naturally. A loaf that stays pillowy-soft for a week probably has additives.

None of these is proof on its own, since bakers vary their styles, but together with the label they tell you a lot.

Does whole grain make a difference?

Yes. Real sourdough made with white flour is still refined bread. Wholegrain or rye sourdough gives you more fibre, vitamins and minerals, and fibre does even more for blood sugar and digestion than fermentation alone. The best combination is real fermentation and whole grains. See Sourdough vs normal bread for more on how the two compare.

What to ask at the bakery

Unpackaged bread from bakeries and supermarket counters often has no ingredient list on display. Don't be shy about asking:

  • Is it made with a sourdough starter only, or is yeast added?
  • How long does the dough ferment?
  • What flour do you use?
  • Are there any additives?

A genuine sourdough baker will usually be proud to tell you.

Your fake sourdough checklist

  1. Check the ingredients for added yeast.
  2. Look for added vinegar, acids or sourdough powder.
  3. Look for a short ingredient list: flour, water, salt and starter.
  4. Check the crust and crumb.
  5. Ask about fermentation time if there's no label.
  6. Choose wholegrain or rye when you can.

Don't trust the label alone

The word "sourdough" on the front isn't always regulated, and some products use it loosely. The ingredients list on the back, and the questions you ask, tell you far more. Real sourdough costs more because it takes time; make sure that's what you're paying for.

The bottom line

Real sourdough is made slowly with a starter and needs only flour, water and salt. Fake sourdough is quick yeasted bread with added sourness. Read the ingredients, look at the loaf, ask questions where there's no label, and choose wholegrain where you can. That way you get the flavour and the possible benefits you're paying for.
