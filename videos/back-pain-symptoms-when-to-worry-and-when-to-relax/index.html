Back pain symptoms: when to worry and when to relax

The full guide

Back pain can be frightening, especially when every search result seems to point to something serious. But the truth most people never hear is that the large majority of back pain isn't dangerous. It hurts, and it can stop you sleeping or even putting on your socks, but it usually comes from muscles, joints and the soft tissues around the spine, and it settles with time and the right care. A few specific signs should never be ignored, though. Here's how to tell the difference, and the daily habits that keep your back healthy.

Most back pain is mechanical, and it gets better

Around nine out of ten cases of low back pain are what doctors call non-specific or mechanical. There's no single damaged structure you could point to on a scan. It's usually a strained muscle, an irritated joint, or a back asked to do something it wasn't ready for: lifting a heavy bag with a twist, sitting for hours, or simply waking up stiff.

This kind of pain has a recognisable pattern:

  • A dull ache or a tight, gripping feeling in the lower back
  • It changes with movement and position
  • It can feel sharp when you bend or stand up from a chair
  • It can be intense for the first few days, then improves steadily

Most episodes are much better within a few weeks, and many people are back to normal within about six. Resting in bed all day isn't the answer. Gentle movement is.

Muscle pain vs nerve pain

When a muscle is the problem, the pain stays in your back. It's local, often on one side, may come with spasms, and the area can feel tender to press.

When a disc is involved or a nerve is squeezed, the pain often travels, running from the buttock down the back of the thigh, sometimes to the foot. This is sciatica, and it can come with tingling, pins and needles or numbness. Leg pain that's worse than the back pain is a classic sign of an irritated nerve.

Sciatica also improves on its own for many people over a few weeks. It becomes more concerning if your leg is getting weaker, your foot starts to drag or slap when you walk, or the numbness keeps spreading. Those are reasons to get checked rather than wait.

Pain at night

Ordinary back pain usually eases when you lie down and find a comfortable position. Doctors take more notice of pain that wakes you from sleep, pain that's just as bad when you're lying completely still, or pain that never eases in any position. On their own, these don't prove anything serious is happening, but they're reasons to see a doctor. "It's probably just my age" should never be the end of the conversation when pain doesn't behave like normal back pain.

The emergency signs

Remember these clearly. Back pain with any of the following can be a sign of cauda equina syndrome, where the bundle of nerves at the bottom of the spinal cord is badly compressed:

  • Problems controlling your bladder or bowels, such as being unable to pee or leaking without realising
  • Numbness around your genitals, inner thighs or buttocks (the "saddle" area)
  • Weakness in both legs

It's rare, but it's a medical emergency. Go to A&E straight away, because speed of treatment can make a real difference to whether those nerves recover.

Signs that point beyond your muscles

Some symptoms move back pain out of the "wait and see" category and into "let's check":

  • Back pain with a fever or feeling generally unwell, which can suggest infection
  • Unexplained weight loss, especially with a history of cancer
  • Back pain starting for the first time after 50 without an obvious cause
  • Long-term steroid use or a weakened immune system

None of these automatically means danger. They simply mean a doctor should look.

Back pain after a fall

If you're older, have osteoporosis, or have broken a bone easily before, even a minor fall can cause a small fracture in one of the bones of the spine. Sometimes a cough, a sneeze or lifting a pot is enough. The pain is often sudden, in one spot in the middle or upper back, and worse when standing or walking. These fractures can be one reason some older adults lose height or become stooped. Get it checked, because treating the bone weakness can help prevent the next one.

Is it my back or my kidney?

  • Location: kidney pain usually sits higher, just under the ribs towards the side, often on one side. Muscle pain tends to be lower, near the belt line.
  • Movement: muscle pain changes when you bend, twist or shift position. Kidney pain usually doesn't.
  • Other symptoms: a kidney infection often brings fever, chills, nausea and burning or frequent urination. Kidney stones can cause severe pain in waves, sometimes moving towards the groin, with blood in the urine.

If back pain comes with urinary symptoms or a fever, don't treat it as a back problem. Get your kidneys checked.

Daily habits that protect your back

  • Break up sitting. Get up every 30 to 45 minutes, even just to walk across the room. Your spine likes movement.
  • Strengthen your core. Your core is the whole ring around your trunk, including back and hips. Strong support muscles take load off the spine.
  • Walk. It's gentle, keeps the back moving and is one of the most underrated treatments for back pain.
  • Use heat early on. It can help tight muscles relax, and guidelines recommend staying as active as you comfortably can.
  • Look after stress and sleep. Stress tightens muscles, low mood can make pain feel bigger, and poor sleep lowers how much pain you can handle.

The bottom line

Most back pain is your body telling you it's been overloaded, not that something is seriously wrong. It needs movement, patience and better daily habits, not panic. But see a doctor for pain that wakes you or never eases, fever, unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer, pain after a fall, or signs pointing to your kidneys. And if you have bladder or bowel problems, saddle numbness or weakness in both legs, go to A&E immediately.
