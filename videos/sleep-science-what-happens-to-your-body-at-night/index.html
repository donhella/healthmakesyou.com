Sleep science - What happens to your body at night?

The full guide

Sleep can feel like switching off for the night, but it's anything but. While you lie there unaware, your brain, heart, muscles, hormones and immune system are all busy with work that can't happen properly while you're awake. Understanding what goes on might change how you think about going to bed late. Here's what your body does at night, and why skimping on sleep catches up with you.

The stages of a night's sleep

Sleep isn't one steady state. Through the night you cycle through stages roughly every 90 minutes: light sleep, deep slow-wave sleep, and REM (rapid eye movement) sleep, when most vivid dreaming happens. Deep sleep is more common in the first half of the night, and REM sleep becomes longer towards the morning. Each stage does different jobs, which is why both the length and the quality of your sleep matter.

What your brain does while you sleep

Your brain is busy sorting the day. During deep sleep and REM sleep, it replays and strengthens new memories and skills, moving them from short-term to longer-term storage. That's why a good night's sleep after learning something helps you remember it.

Your brain also cleans house. In 2013, researchers published a study in the journal Science showing that in mice, the space between brain cells widened during sleep, allowing fluid to flush out waste products much faster than during waking hours. One of those waste products is beta-amyloid, a protein linked to Alzheimer's disease. Research in humans is still developing, but it's one more reason sleep matters for long-term brain health.

Why your heart needs the night

During deep sleep, your heart rate slows and your blood pressure falls by around 10 to 20%, a pattern known as "dipping". It gives your heart and blood vessels a nightly rest.

When sleep is regularly short or broken, that rest is cut short, and blood pressure stays higher for more of the day and night. Over time, poor sleep is linked with high blood pressure, heart disease and stroke. Sleep apnoea, where breathing repeatedly stops during the night, is a particular strain on the heart. Loud snoring, gasping in the night and daytime sleepiness are worth mentioning to a doctor.

How your body recovers overnight

Deep sleep is when the body releases much of its growth hormone, which helps repair muscles, tissues and bones. It's when the damage from the day's activity gets fixed, which is why athletes, and anyone recovering from illness or injury, need good sleep. Skin cells and other tissues also renew more actively overnight.

Sleep, hormones and metabolism

Sleep has a powerful effect on the hormones that control hunger and blood sugar. A well-known 1999 study in The Lancet found that when healthy young men were limited to four hours of sleep a night for less than a week, their bodies handled glucose much less effectively, in a pattern resembling the early stages of diabetes. Their stress hormone levels also changed.

Short sleep also shifts the balance between two appetite hormones: ghrelin, which makes you hungry, goes up, and leptin, which signals fullness, goes down. That helps explain why a bad night often leads to cravings for sugary, high-calorie foods, and why regular short sleep is linked to weight gain.

Sleep and your immune system

While you sleep, your immune system releases proteins called cytokines that help fight infection and inflammation, and it strengthens its memory of germs it has met. A 2015 study in the journal Sleep exposed volunteers to a cold virus: people who slept less than six hours a night were about four times more likely to catch the cold than those who slept more than seven.

How much sleep do you need?

Most adults need seven to nine hours a night, and older adults around seven to eight. Teenagers need more. Needs vary a little between people, but regularly getting less than seven hours is linked to more health problems.

Habits that help

  • Keep regular times. Go to bed and get up at roughly the same time, even at weekends.
  • Get daylight early. Morning light helps set your body clock.
  • Watch caffeine. Avoid it from early afternoon.
  • Dim the lights and screens in the last hour before bed.
  • Keep the bedroom cool, dark and quiet.
  • Go easy on alcohol. It may help you drop off, but it disrupts deeper sleep later in the night.
  • Move during the day. Regular activity helps you sleep more deeply.

If you regularly struggle to sleep, wake unrefreshed, snore loudly or feel very sleepy during the day, talk to your GP.

Putting it all together

Every night, sleep sorts and stores your memories, clears waste from your brain, rests your heart, repairs your body, balances the hormones that control hunger and blood sugar, and strengthens your immune system. Short-changing it doesn't just leave you tired; it quietly affects almost every system in your body. Treat sleep as part of your health routine, not the time left over at the end of the day.
