Your health is a game of chess and most people are already losing

The full guide

Your health works a lot like a game of chess. Good players don't just think about the move in front of them; they think about where it leads several moves later. Health is the same. The choices you make today rarely show their effects straight away, but they shape where you'll be in ten, twenty or thirty years. Here are the everyday moves that quietly add up, the signs and numbers worth paying attention to, and why thinking a few moves ahead makes such a difference.

The moves that quietly add up

Most health problems don't arrive overnight. Type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease and many joint problems build slowly, through thousands of small, repeated choices: what's on your plate, how much you move, how you sleep, whether you smoke, how much you drink.

No single meal or lazy day matters much on its own. It's the pattern that counts. In chess, one weak move can be recovered from; a long series of them leaves you with no good options. The encouraging side is that the same is true in reverse. Small good moves, repeated, build a strong position.

What happens when you stop moving

Movement is one of the most powerful moves on the board. When we stop moving, muscles weaken, joints stiffen, bones lose strength, blood sugar control worsens and the heart gets less fit. From middle age, we naturally lose muscle each year, and inactivity speeds that up.

The NHS recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as brisk walking, plus strength exercises on two days. You don't need a gym. Walking, climbing stairs, gardening, carrying shopping and standing up from a chair repeatedly all count. The key is consistency, and breaking up long periods of sitting.

Protecting your energy

Energy is a resource, like pieces on a board. Poor sleep, constant stress, skipped meals and too much caffeine or alcohol all drain it. When you're running on empty, it's harder to make good choices about food and exercise, so one weak move leads to the next. Protecting your sleep, eating regular balanced meals and finding ways to manage stress keep your energy available for the moves that matter.

Small signs you should not ignore

Your body often gives early warnings. In chess, ignoring a small threat can cost you the game. In health, these signs deserve attention:

  • Feeling unusually tired for weeks
  • Being very thirsty or needing to pee more often
  • Unexplained weight loss or gain
  • Getting out of breath more easily than before
  • Changes in your bowel habits, or blood in your poo or pee
  • A lump, a new mole or a mole that's changing
  • Persistent pain, cough or heartburn

Most of the time these turn out to be minor, but they're worth getting checked rather than explaining away.

Numbers worth knowing

A few simple numbers tell you a lot about where you stand:

  • Blood pressure: ideally below 120/80. High blood pressure usually has no symptoms, so the only way to know is to measure it.
  • Waist size: a waist over 94 cm (37 inches) for men or 80 cm (31.5 inches) for women is linked to higher health risks, and the risk is higher still for some ethnic groups.
  • Blood sugar or HbA1c: catches prediabetes early, when it's easiest to reverse.
  • Cholesterol: especially if heart disease runs in your family.
  • Body mass index (BMI): a rough guide to whether your weight is in a healthy range.

Many pharmacies offer free blood pressure checks, and in many countries adults in midlife can get a regular health check from their doctor.

Thinking a few moves ahead

Ask yourself: if I keep doing what I'm doing now, where will I be in ten years? Will my knees, heart, lungs and blood sugar thank me? That question can change today's choices without any need for drastic overhauls. It's not about fear. It's about strategy.

Why small choices matter

Big, sudden changes rarely last. Small changes do. A daily 20-minute walk, an extra portion of vegetables, one less sugary drink, going to bed half an hour earlier. Each is easy on its own, but over months and years they compound into a very different outcome.

Your habits tell the story

In chess, you can often read a player's strategy from their moves. Your habits tell a similar story about your future health. The good news is that the story isn't finished. At almost any age, improving your activity, diet, sleep, and stopping smoking brings real benefits.

The bigger picture

Health isn't about being perfect or never enjoying yourself. It's about making more good moves than bad ones, noticing threats early, and keeping your future self in mind. Play the long game, and you give yourself the best chance of staying strong, independent and active for many years.
