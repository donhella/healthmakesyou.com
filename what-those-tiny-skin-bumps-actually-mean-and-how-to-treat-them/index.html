What those tiny skin bumps actually mean (and how to treat them)

Rough bumps on your arms, white dots under your eyes, red spots after shaving or itchy raised patches: what the five most common types of tiny skin bumps are, and the simple care that helps each one.

By Jude UchellaFounder, Health makes you
26 Nov 20267 min read

Almost everyone has noticed them at some point: small bumps on the backs of the arms, a scatter of tiny white dots near the eyes, red spots that show up a day after shaving, or raised itchy patches that appear out of nowhere. Most of the time they don't hurt. They're just annoying, and it's hard to know whether to scrub them, squeeze them or leave them alone.

The good news is that most tiny skin bumps fall into a handful of common, harmless groups. Once you know which one you're looking at, the right care is usually simple. Here are the five you're most likely to see, what's behind each one, and what actually helps.

Keratosis pilaris: the "chicken skin" bumps

If you have small, rough bumps on the backs of your upper arms, your thighs or your buttocks, and your skin feels like sandpaper there, it's very likely keratosis pilaris. The bumps can be skin-coloured, red or slightly brown, and they tend to look worse in winter when the air is dry.

The NHS describes keratosis pilaris as a common, harmless condition caused by a build-up of keratin, a protein in the skin, which plugs the hair follicles. It often runs in families and is especially common in teenagers and in people with dry skin or eczema. It isn't an infection, and it isn't caused by poor hygiene.

There's no permanent cure, but the bumps often fade with age, and gentle daily care makes the skin feel much smoother. The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) suggests:

  • Wash gently. Use a mild soap-free cleanser and warm (not hot) water, and keep showers short.
  • Moisturise straight after washing, while the skin is still a little damp, and again during the day.
  • Choose a cream with a gentle exfoliating ingredient, such as urea, lactic acid or salicylic acid. These soften the plugs over time.
  • Don't scrub hard. Rough loofahs and harsh scrubs irritate the skin and can make the redness worse.
  • Use a humidifier in dry, heated rooms in winter.

Expect it to take several weeks of steady use before you see a difference, and keep going once it improves, because the bumps usually come back if you stop.

Acne and clogged pores: not always the big, obvious spots

Not all acne looks like large, painful spots. Very often it shows up as clusters of small bumps: blackheads, whiteheads and tiny red spots on the forehead, cheeks, chin, chest or back. These start when oil and dead skin cells clog a pore. When bacteria and inflammation join in, the bump turns red and sore.

Acne is extremely common in teenagers, but plenty of adults get it too, especially women around their periods, during pregnancy or when changing contraception. Stress, heavy or oily skincare products and sweaty kit left on after exercise can make it worse.

For mild acne, the AAD and the NHS both recommend going back to basics:

  • Wash twice a day and after sweating, using a gentle cleanser and your fingertips rather than a rough cloth.
  • Look for over-the-counter products with benzoyl peroxide, salicylic acid or adapalene. Start slowly, every other night at first, because they can dry the skin.
  • Choose products labelled "non-comedogenic" or "oil-free", which are less likely to block pores.
  • Don't squeeze or pick. It pushes the blockage deeper and raises the chance of dark marks and scars.
  • Give a new routine 6 to 8 weeks before you decide it isn't working.

If your acne is painful, leaving scars, or affecting how you feel about yourself, see a GP, pharmacist or dermatologist. Prescription treatments work well and are worth asking about early.

Milia: tiny white "pearls" under the skin

Milia are small, firm, white or yellowish dots, usually 1 to 2 millimetres across, that sit just under the surface of the skin. They're most common around the eyes, on the eyelids, cheeks and nose. Unlike whiteheads, they aren't connected to a pore, so there's nothing to "pop".

Milia form when keratin gets trapped in a tiny pocket under the skin. They're very common in newborn babies, where they usually clear by themselves within a few weeks. In adults, they can follow sun damage, heavy creams around the eyes, or skin that has been burned, blistered or treated with lasers or strong peels.

The most important rule with milia is not to squeeze them. Because they're sealed under the skin, pressing or digging at them usually just damages the skin and can leave a mark. Instead:

  • Gently exfoliate the area a few times a week with a mild chemical exfoliant, keeping well away from the eye itself.
  • Switch to lighter eye creams and moisturisers, and avoid very heavy, greasy products on the face.
  • Wear sunscreen every day, because sun-damaged skin is more prone to milia.
  • Be patient. Many milia clear on their own over a few months.

If they don't go, or they bother you, a dermatologist can remove them quickly and safely with a sterile needle or a small extraction tool. This isn't something to try at home, especially near the eyes.

Folliculitis: red, itchy bumps around hairs

Folliculitis is inflammation of the hair follicles. It looks like small red bumps or little white-headed pimples around hairs, and it can itch, sting or feel tender. It often shows up on the legs, beard area, bikini line, buttocks, chest or back.

According to the AAD, common triggers include shaving, waxing, tight clothing that rubs, heavy sweating, and soaking in a hot tub or pool that isn't properly cleaned. Bacteria are often involved, and less often yeast or fungi.

Mild folliculitis usually clears on its own within a week or two if you stop irritating the area. To help it along and stop it coming back:

  • Use a warm, damp cloth on the bumps for 10 to 15 minutes a few times a day to soothe them.
  • Wash with a gentle antibacterial cleanser and pat the skin dry.
  • Take a break from shaving the area, and when you start again, use a clean, sharp razor, shave in the direction of hair growth and don't press hard.
  • Wear loose, breathable clothes, and change out of sweaty clothes soon after exercise.
  • Don't share razors or towels.

See a doctor if the bumps spread, become large and painful, keep coming back, or you develop a fever. You may need a prescription cream or tablets.

Allergic and contact reactions: when your skin pushes back

Sometimes bumps appear because your skin is reacting to something. There are two common types.

Hives are raised, itchy welts or patches that can appear anywhere, change shape and move around the body, often fading within hours and popping up somewhere else. The NHS lists triggers such as certain foods, medicines, insect stings, heat, stress and infections, although often no clear trigger is ever found.

Contact dermatitis is a red, itchy and sometimes bumpy or blistered rash that shows up where your skin has touched something it doesn't like. Common culprits include fragrance, hair dye, nickel in jewellery and belt buckles, some skincare ingredients, cleaning products, and plants.

To track down and calm a reaction:

  • Think about what's new. A new soap, washing powder, perfume, skincare product, food or medicine is a good place to start.
  • Look at where the rash is. A rash under a watch strap, on the earlobes or around the neck points to something that touches that spot.
  • Stop using the suspected product and see if the skin settles over a week or two.
  • Use a cool compress and a fragrance-free moisturiser, and try an over-the-counter antihistamine for itchy hives. A pharmacist can advise you.
  • Keep a simple diary if reactions keep coming back, noting what you ate, used and did before each one.

Get urgent medical help if hives come with swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, difficulty breathing, or feeling faint. These can be signs of a serious allergic reaction.

When to get your skin checked

Most small bumps are harmless, but it's worth seeing a doctor or pharmacist if:

  • a bump is growing, bleeding, changing colour or shape, or doesn't heal
  • bumps are very painful, hot, swollen or oozing pus
  • you have a fever or feel unwell alongside a rash
  • the bumps keep coming back or are affecting your sleep or confidence
  • home care hasn't helped after a few weeks

A quick look from a professional can tell you exactly what you're dealing with and save you weeks of trying the wrong products.

The bottom line

Those tiny bumps usually have a simple explanation: plugged follicles in keratosis pilaris, clogged pores in acne, trapped keratin in milia, irritated hair follicles in folliculitis, or a reaction to something your skin has touched. Gentle washing, regular moisturising, the right active ingredient and leaving the bumps alone instead of picking at them go a long way. And if something looks unusual or won't settle, get it checked.
